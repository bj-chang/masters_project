\begin{abstract}
\noindent This dissertation studies mesh dependence in PDE-constrained optimisation. The number of iterations a gradient-based optimiser needs can stay flat as the computational mesh is refined, or grow without bound. Which of the two happens depends on the inner product the optimiser uses to turn a derivative into a gradient. We first derive and verify the adjoint machinery for two model problems, the linear Poisson equation and the nonlinear, time-dependent viscous Burgers equation, using manufactured solutions and Taylor tests. We then reproduce the central result of \citet{shfp2017}. Respecting the function-space $L^{2}$ inner product through its Riesz map makes a quasi-Newton optimiser essentially mesh-independent, while the Euclidean inner product used by default in most optimisation packages does not. A survey of the optimisers in PETSc TAO establishes which of them honour that inner product, and a comparison of four L-BFGS implementations at source level explains why one of them appeared not to. The main contribution of this work is to show that the same mesh dependence appears again one level deeper, in the inner Krylov solve that a Newton optimiser uses to compute each step. We remove it there with the same Riesz map, applied as a preconditioner. The inner iteration count falls from over a thousand to a constant twelve across the mesh sweep. That an inner product can act as a preconditioner is already present in classical operator-preconditioning theory and in ongoing PETSc/TAO development. We appear to be the first to expose and remove the effect experimentally in an adjoint optimisation pipeline. The metric has to be supplied at both the outer and the inner level. We explain spectrally why the Riesz map is the right preconditioner, show that it must match the norm in which the control is regularised, and identify one solver that neither mechanism can reach. A production version of the preconditioner has been merged into the pyadjoint library.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

Many problems in science and engineering involve determining unknown inputs to a physical system based on partial or indirect observations of its output. These sorts of problems are commonly formulated as PDE-constrained optimisation problems, in which the behaviour of the system is governed by a partial differential equation, while the unknown input is found by minimising a suitable objective functional.

The governing equations in the problems we consider are partial differential equations, and one widely used way to solve them on a computer is the finite element method. The idea is to replace the continuous domain by a \emph{mesh}, a partition of it into many small cells, here triangles, and to approximate the unknown function by a simple polynomial on each cell. This turns the differential equation into a finite system of linear equations, with roughly one unknown per mesh vertex, which a computer can solve directly, and the approximation improves as the mesh is made finer. Importantly for what follows, practical meshes are deliberately \emph{non-uniform}, in that they are refined where the solution changes rapidly and left coarse elsewhere. That a mesh can be graded in this way, with small cells in some regions and large cells in others, is exactly what makes mesh-dependence a phenomenon worth studying.

Solving an optimisation problem on top of such a finite element model needs several ingredients, which we build in order. The first is a \emph{forward solve}, which, given a trial control, solves the PDE and returns the resulting state. The second is the \emph{gradient} of the objective with respect to the control, which the optimiser needs in order to know which way to step; computing it directly, one direction at a time, would be far too expensive, so we use the \emph{adjoint} method, which recovers the entire gradient from a single additional solve. The third is the \emph{optimiser} itself, which uses the gradient to iterate towards the best control. Each of these has to be built and, just as importantly, verified before it can be trusted, which is why the first half of this dissertation derives and tests the forward solve, the adjoint and the gradient on two model problems before any mesh-dependence experiment is attempted. The final ingredient, and the one the whole story turns on, is the \emph{inner product} the optimiser uses to turn the gradient into a search direction.

Such problems are solved iteratively, and one would expect that refining the computational mesh simply sharpens the answer without changing the character of the optimisation itself. This expectation can fail dramatically. On the same problem, solved by the same quasi-Newton method to the same tolerance, the number of iterations needed can either stay essentially constant as the mesh is refined or grow without bound -- in the experiments of this dissertation, a flat count of around six against a count that doubles at every refinement, reaching well over a thousand. The difference is not the problem, the algorithm, or the mesh, but a single choice buried inside the optimiser, namely the inner product it uses to turn a derivative into a gradient. Most optimisation packages are hard-wired to the Euclidean inner product of the coefficient vector, which is the wrong choice on a non-uniform mesh. The right choice is the inner product of the function space in which the control actually lives, applied through its Riesz map. This dissertation is really the story of that one choice.

We approach it in three stages. First, we build and verify the adjoint-based optimisation machinery on two model problems, the linear Poisson equation and the nonlinear, time-dependent Burgers equation, so that the later experiments rest on a pipeline whose forward solves and gradients are known to be correct. Second, we reproduce the central mesh-dependence result of \citet{shfp2017}, namely that respecting the function-space inner product makes a quasi-Newton optimiser essentially mesh-independent, while the Euclidean inner product does not. Third, we expose the same mesh-dependence one level deeper, in the inner Krylov solve that a Newton optimiser uses to compute each step, and remove it by the same idea applied there, as a preconditioner. That the inner product plays the role of a preconditioner is anticipated by classical operator preconditioning \citep{kirby2010, mardal2011} and has been discussed among the PETSc/TAO developers \citep{petsctao_discord2025}; the contribution here is to expose and remove the effect experimentally in an adjoint optimisation pipeline, to explain it spectrally, and to contribute a production preconditioner that has since been merged into pyadjoint. The metric has to be put in at both levels.

The dissertation is organised as follows. \Cref{sec:preliminaries} collects the functional-analytic background (Sobolev spaces, dual spaces, and the Riesz representation theorem) on which the whole argument rests. \Cref{sec:poisson-weak,sec:poisson-optimisation,sec:poisson-derivative,sec:poisson-numerics} take the linear Poisson problem through its weak form, its optimal-control formulation, the adjoint derivation of the gradient, and numerical verification, and \cref{sec:burgers-weak,sec:burgers-derivative,sec:burgers-numerics} repeat the same programme for the nonlinear Burgers problem. \Cref{sec:mesh-dependence} reproduces the mesh-dependence experiment, \cref{sec:lbfgs-comparison} traces at the level of source code exactly where the inner product enters each limited-memory optimiser, and \cref{sec:precond} develops the two-level preconditioning contribution. \Cref{sec:conclusions} concludes.

\subsection{Contributions}

The novel contributions of this dissertation are the following.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A systematic determination, across a mesh-refinement sweep spanning ratios from four to $128$, of which of the optimisation algorithms in the current PETSc TAO library honour the function-space inner product and which do not.
  \item Verification that TAO's limited-memory quasi-Newton method honours the inner product through its initial-Hessian seed and is mesh-independent at either history length, with the history setting only the size of the flat count; this resolves an apparent failure to reproduce the reference results of \citet{shfp2017} that had earlier been attributed to an internal Euclidean inner product.
  \item A demonstration that the Newton line-search method is mesh-independent in its outer iterations while the mesh-dependence reappears in its inner Krylov solve, and that preconditioning that inner solve with the Riesz map removes it, reducing the inner iteration count from over a thousand to a constant across the mesh sweep.
  \item The principal contribution of this work is a production-quality implementation of that inner preconditioner, \texttt{RieszMapPC}. Realised as a PETSc Python preconditioner, it applies the Riesz map of each pyadjoint \texttt{Control} to the reduced-Hessian Krylov solve inside TAO's Newton method, reading the metric directly from the \texttt{Control} so that a user obtains a mesh-independent inner solve simply by attaching a Riesz map to the control, with none of the manual plumbing of the research prototype. It has been merged into pyadjoint, so the two-level fix is available to every user of the stack rather than confined to this dissertation. The Firedrake regression test that exercises it end to end has been merged in turn. To our knowledge it is the first implementation to expose and remove this inner-level mesh-dependence in an adjoint optimisation pipeline.
  \item A diagnosis of the two remaining mesh-dependent algorithms, namely the bounded quasi-Newton line-search method, which is curable by supplying the same seed by hand (an effect we isolate in a controlled experiment and explain through the spectrum of the reduced Hessian in the two inner products), and nonlinear conjugate gradient, which is shown to be structurally incurable within TAO for want of any interface at which the metric could be supplied.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Mathematical Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}
% =============================================================================

Before we can derive the adjoint equation, discretise the optimisation problem, or talk about what ``mesh-independent'' even means, we need to establish some background, from the equations themselves to the functional-analytic objects that the mesh-dependence story rests on. Here we loosely follow the first chapter of \citet{shfp2017}.

\subsection{Partial differential equations}
\label{sec:prelim:pde}

First, we need the definition of a PDE. A PDE (partial differential equation) is an equation that relates an unknown function of several variables to its partial derivatives. For example we can have: 
\begin{itemize}
\item The heat equation $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$, which describes how a temperature $u(x, t)$ spreads along a rod over time.
\item The wave equation $\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = c^2\, \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$, which describes the displacement $u(x, t)$ of a vibrating string.
\end{itemize}

The two main model problems in this dissertation are Poisson's equation $-\Delta u = m$, where $\Delta$ is the Laplacian (the sum of the second partial derivatives), and the viscous Burgers equation. Poisson's equation has no time dependence and so describes a steady state, while the Burgers equation is nonlinear and time-dependent. 

\subsection{The PDE-constrained optimisation problem}
\label{sec:prelim:problem}

We are interested in problems where we want to recover an unknown input to a physical system from indirect observations of its output. So, it is useful for us to fix the terminology that we will use to describe this class of problems. Every problem we study is built from the following four components:

The first is the \emph{state variable} $u$. This is the quantity that describes the response of the physical system. 

The second is the \emph{control variable} $m$. This is the input that we are free to vary, such as a source term, a boundary value, or a coefficient. The choice of control is what distinguishes one instance of the system from another, and this is the variable that we want to optimise over.

The third is the \emph{state equation}, the PDE that governs the system and links the state to the control. For each admissible control $m$, the state equation determines a corresponding state $u$. In this sense, the state is a function of the control (we make this dependence precise later using the solution operator of \cref{defn:solution-operator}).

The final component is the \emph{objective functional} (or cost functional) $J(u, m)$, which measures how good a given state and control pair is. It usually penalises the mismatch between the state $u$ and a prescribed desired state $d$, together with the size of the control. A \emph{PDE-constrained optimisation problem} is then the problem of choosing the control $m$ that minimises $J(u, m)$, subject to the constraint that $u$ and $m$ satisfy the state equation.

\subsection{Domain and function spaces}
\label{sec:prelim:spaces}

Since a PDE is an equation about a function on a domain, we must first define both functions and domains properly. Poisson's equation and the other elliptic problems of this dissertation\footnote{Informally, the elliptic PDEs are the steady problems, with Poisson's equation the prototype -- the precise definition involves the second-order terms and is not needed here.} can be posed in the classical setting, where solutions are smooth enough to differentiate pointwise everywhere. We choose instead to work in a weaker setting, because we want to admit less regular functions, whose derivatives are defined only in a weak sense, by integration against test functions\footnote{A test function here is just a smooth function that we are free to choose, and the weak derivative is defined by asking the usual integration-by-parts formula to hold against every such test function, rather than by differentiating at each point. We show exactly how this works for Poisson's equation in \cref{sec:poisson-weak:derivation}; for now it is enough to know that it lets us make sense of derivatives for functions that are not smooth.}. So, we need a function space which is large enough to contain them. The Sobolev spaces $H^1(\Om)$ and $H_0^1(\Om)$ give us exactly this~\citep[Sect.~5.2]{hc2026}.

Let $\Om \subset \R^d$, with $d \in \{1, 2, 3\}$, be a bounded open domain with Lipschitz boundary $\dom$.\footnote{Informally, a Lipschitz boundary is one that is not too rough, in the sense that corners like a polygon's are allowed, but not cusps or fractal-like features. The unit square used in the experiments qualifies.} We assume that all functions are defined on $\Om$, unless stated otherwise. We denote by $\Lp{\Om}$ the space of square-integrable functions on $\Om$, equipped with the inner product and norm
\begin{equation}
  \inner{u}{v}{\Lp{\Om}} := \int_{\Om} u v \dx,
  \qquad
  \|u\|_{\Lp{\Om}} := \inner{u}{u}{\Lp{\Om}}^{1/2}.
\end{equation}

\begin{defn}[Sobolev space \texorpdfstring{$H^1(\Om)$}{H1}] The \emph{Sobolev space} $H^1(\Om)$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
  H^1(\Om) := \left\{ u \in \Lp{\Om} \;\middle|\;
                      \grad u \in (\Lp{\Om})^d \right\}.
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

So, we have that a function $u$ belongs to $H^1(\Om)$ if both the function itself and its first (weak) derivatives are square-integrable. This space is equipped with the norm
\begin{equation}
  \|u\|_{H^1(\Om)}^2
    := \|u\|_{\Lp{\Om}}^2 + \|\grad u\|_{\Lp{\Om}}^2.
\end{equation}

This norm comes from the $H^1$ inner product,
\begin{equation}
  \inner{u}{v}{H^1(\Om)} := \inner{u}{v}{\Lp{\Om}} + \inner{\grad u}{\grad v}{\Lp{\Om}}
    = \int_{\Om} u v \dx + \int_{\Om} \grad u \cdot \grad v \dx.
\end{equation}

To also incorporate homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions into the function space itself, we also want to introduce the subspace $H_0^1(\Om)$.

\begin{defn}[Sobolev space \texorpdfstring{$H_0^1(\Om)$}{H10}] The \emph{Sobolev space} $H_0^1(\Om)$ is defined as the closure of $C_c^\infty(\Om)$ in the $H^1(\Om)$ norm.
\end{defn}

Here, $C_c^\infty(\Om)$ denotes the smooth functions with compact support in $\Om$ (i.e., smooth functions which vanish in a neighbourhood of $\dom$). Taking the \emph{closure} of this set in the $H^1$ norm means we also include every function that can be approximated arbitrarily well, in the $H^1$ norm, by such smooth functions. Functions in $H_0^1(\Om)$ are zero on the boundary $\dom$, which makes this the natural solution space for problems with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions.

They also satisfy the following inequality, which we can use to show well-posedness.
\begin{theorem}[Poincar\'e inequality]
\label{thm:poincare}
There is a constant $C_\Om > 0$, depending only on $\Om$, such that $\| u \|_{\Lp{\Om}} \le C_\Om\, \| \grad u \|_{\Lp{\Om}}$ for all $u \in H_0^1(\Om)$~\citep{bs2008}.
\end{theorem}

The spaces $\Lp{\Om}$, $H^1(\Om)$ and $H_0^1(\Om)$ defined above are all examples of \emph{Hilbert spaces}, and the mesh-dependence story depends on the choice of inner product that is placed on such a space. We record the two underlying notions precisely.

\begin{defn}[Inner product]
An \emph{inner product} on a real vector space $H$ is a map $(\cdot, \cdot) : H \times H \to \R$. More precisely, let $u, v, w \in H$ be vectors and $\alpha \in \R$ a scalar; then it satisfies the following four properties:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $(u + v, w) = (u, w) + (v, w)$ \hfill (additivity)
  \item $(\alpha v, w) = \alpha\, (v, w)$ \hfill (homogeneity)
  \item $(v, w) = (w, v)$ \hfill (symmetry)
  \item $(v, v) \ge 0$, with equality if and only if $v = 0$ \hfill (positive definiteness)
\end{enumerate}
An inner product induces the norm $\|v\| := (v, v)^{1/2}$.
\end{defn}

\begin{defn}[Hilbert space]
A \emph{Hilbert space} is an inner product space that is \emph{complete}, meaning every Cauchy sequence has a limit in the space.
\end{defn}

Throughout the dissertation, the control variable $m$ lives in a Hilbert space $\Mspace$, the control space, equipped with an inner product $\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}{\Mspace}$ and associated norm $\|\cdot\|_{\Mspace}$.

\subsection{Dual spaces and the Riesz representation theorem}
\label{sec:prelim:riesz}

As we see later in this dissertation, the mesh-dependence story here is, at its root, a story about a choice of inner product. For an inner product to be the subject of a choice, we first need to understand why we have any freedom to choose this at all. This requires understanding the relationship between a Hilbert space and its dual. The derivative of a real-valued functional on a Hilbert space is naturally an element of the dual space (a linear functional), but actually in practice we want to picture it as an element of the original space (``a gradient''). So, we introduce the Riesz representation theorem which provides a canonical bijection between the two views. This bijection depends on the choice of inner product, and that dependence is exactly what the mesh-dependence study in \cref{sec:mesh-dependence} depends on. Here we set up the statements that we need, and the consequences are described in \cref{sec:mesh-dependence}.

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space with inner product $\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}{H}$.

\begin{defn}[Dual space] The \emph{dual space} of $H$, denoted $H^{*}$, is the space of all continuous linear functionals on $H$ \citep[p.~21]{shfp2017}:
\begin{equation}
  H^{*} := \bigl\{ f : H \to \R \;\big|\;
                    f \text{ is linear and continuous} \bigr\}.
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

We write duality pairings between $H^{*}$ and $H$ in angle brackets, $\langle f, v \rangle$, and inner products in $H$ in parentheses, $(u, v)_H$, so that the bracket style can tell the reader which kind of object lives on each side. One specific case that we will use throughout the dissertation is the Sobolev dual $H^{-1}(\Om) := H_0^1(\Om)^{*}$.

Although $H$ and $H^{*}$ are different spaces of different objects, the Riesz representation theorem below gives a canonical bijection between them~\citep[Theorem~1.1]{shfp2017}.

\begin{theorem}[Riesz representation theorem]
\label{thm:riesz} Let $H$ be a Hilbert space with inner product $\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}{H}$. For any continuous linear functional $f \in H^{*}$, there exists a unique $u_f \in H$ such that
\begin{equation}
  f(v) = \inner{u_f}{v}{H}
  \qquad \forall v \in H,
\end{equation}
and the norms agree, i.e. $\|f\|_{H^{*}} = \|u_f\|_H$.
\end{theorem}

This bijection is what lets us pass between the dual view and the primal view of a linear functional, and the map that effects the bijection is called the Riesz map:

\begin{defn}[Riesz map and Riesz representer]
\label{defn:rieszmap} The map $\Rmap_H : H^{*} \to H$ that sends each $f \in H^{*}$ to its unique representative $u_f \in H$ is called the \emph{Riesz map}. The element $\Rmap_H(f) := u_f$ is called the \emph{Riesz representer} of $f$ in $H$.
\end{defn}

The Riesz map depends on the choice of inner product on $H$ - indeed, the same linear functional has different Riesz representers under different inner products. 

\subsection{Differential calculus in function spaces}
\label{sec:prelim:frechet}

To minimise a functional, we have to differentiate it, and the adjoint method in particular depends on the chain rule applied to a composite operator. In finite dimensions, partial derivatives stacked into a vector are enough. However, the optimisation variable here lives in an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, so we even have to be careful about what ``direction'' actually means. So, we define the Fr{\'e}chet derivative as the right replacement for the finite-dimensional gradient, namely a bounded linear operator\footnote{A linear map between the two spaces whose output norm is at most a fixed multiple of the input norm.} that captures the first-order change of a map between Hilbert spaces~\citep[Sect.~1.2.2--1.2.3]{shfp2017}.

Let $U$ and $V$ be Hilbert spaces, and let $F : U \to V$ be a (possibly nonlinear) operator.

\begin{defn}[Fr{\'e}chet derivative]
\label{defn:frechet} The operator $F$ is \emph{Fr{\'e}chet differentiable} at $u \in U$ if there exists a bounded linear operator $\mathrm{d} F(u) \in \mathcal{L}(U, V)$, where $\mathcal{L}(U, V)$ denotes the space of bounded linear operators from $U$ to $V$, such that
\begin{equation}
  \lim_{\|\delta u\|_U \to 0}
    \frac{\|F(u + \delta u) - F(u) - \mathrm{d} F(u)\, \delta u\|_V}
         {\|\delta u\|_U}
  = 0.
\end{equation}
The directional derivative in the direction $\delta u \in U$ is written $\mathrm{d} F(u; \delta u) := \mathrm{d} F(u)\, \delta u$.
\end{defn}

When an operator or functional depends on multiple variables, we can also naturally extend the idea of Fr{\'e}chet differentiation to partial derivatives.

\begin{defn}[Partial Fr{\'e}chet derivative]
\label{defn:partial-frechet} Let $F : U \times \Mspace \to V$. The \emph{partial Fr{\'e}chet derivative} of $F$ with respect to $u$ at the point $(u, m)$, acting in the direction $\delta u \in U$, is denoted as $\partial_u F(u, m; \delta u) \in V$. It is defined as the Fr{\'e}chet derivative of the map $u \mapsto F(u, m)$ with fixed $m$. The partial derivative with respect to $m$, denoted $\partial_m F(u, m; \delta m)$, is defined analogously.
\end{defn}

Finally, we can get the derivative of a composite functional from the chain rule.

\begin{theorem}[Chain rule]
\label{thm:chain} Let $\Mspace$ and $U$ be Hilbert spaces. Let $S : \Mspace \to U$ be Fr{\'e}chet differentiable at $m \in \Mspace$, and let $J : U \times \Mspace \to \R$ be continuously Fr{\'e}chet differentiable at $(S(m), m)$. Then the reduced functional $\Jt(m) := J(S(m), m)$ is Fr{\'e}chet differentiable at $m$, and its derivative in the direction $\delta m \in \Mspace$ is given by
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{d} \Jt(m;\, \delta m)
  = \partial_u J\bigl( S(m), m;\, \mathrm{d} S(m;\, \delta m) \bigr)
  + \partial_m J\bigl( S(m), m;\, \delta m \bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Bilinear forms and weak formulations}
\label{sec:prelim:weakforms}

Even with the function spaces and calculus in place, the PDEs we want to solve are still written in their classical, pointwise form, but this form requires too much smoothness from the solution. The standard fix is to multiply by a test function and integrate by parts, moving derivatives off the solution and onto the test function. The reformulated equation (called the \emph{weak} or \emph{variational} form) is exactly what the finite element method discretises, and whether it exists or not is determined by the bilinear form that comes from it. The Lax-Milgram theorem turns two simple conditions on that bilinear form (continuity and coercivity) into existence and uniqueness of a weak solution. This transfer of derivatives is useful as it lets us look for solutions in Sobolev spaces instead of in spaces of smooth functions. 

Let $V$ be a Hilbert space. A bilinear form $a(\cdot, \cdot) : V \times V \to \R$ is \emph{continuous} if there exists a constant $C > 0$ such that
\begin{equation}
  |a(u, v)| \le C \|u\|_V \|v\|_V
  \qquad \forall u, v \in V,
\end{equation}
and \emph{coercive} if there exists a constant $\alpha > 0$ such that
\begin{equation}
  a(u, u) \ge \alpha \|u\|_V^2
  \qquad \forall u \in V.
\end{equation}
These two properties are the hypotheses of the following Lax-Milgram theorem~\citep[Theorem~4.36]{hc2026}.

\begin{theorem}[Lax-Milgram]
Let $V$ be a Hilbert space and let $a(\cdot, \cdot) : V \times V \to \R$ be a continuous, coercive bilinear form. Then for every continuous linear functional $\ell \in V^{*}$ there exists a unique $u \in V$ such that
\begin{equation}
  a(u, v) = \ell(v) \qquad \forall v \in V.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

We use this existence and uniqueness later, where it makes the solution operator $S : m \mapsto u$ of \cref{defn:solution-operator} well-defined, and hence the reduced functional we minimise.

% =============================================================================
\section{The Forward Poisson Problem}
\label{sec:poisson-weak}
% =============================================================================

With the function spaces, weak formulations, and the Lax-Milgram theorem now in place, we are ready to study the first of our two model problems, the Poisson equation. We look at this first as it is a relatively simple problem -- one that is linear, and elliptic, with no time dependence. This makes it the natural setting in which we can build up a PDE-constrained optimisation problem (the forward solve, the adjoint equation, the reduced gradient, and the numerical optimisation) one piece at a time, before we repeat the same programme for the harder, nonlinear Burgers equation in \cref{sec:burgers-weak}. It is also natural to begin here because the existence and uniqueness of a solution follow directly from the Lax-Milgram theorem we have just stated. In this section we take the first step, the \emph{forward} problem: for a given control (here the source term $m$) we solve for the corresponding state $u$, leaving the optimisation over $m$ to \cref{sec:poisson-optimisation}.

Our goal is to rewrite the PDE in a form that involves only first derivatives of the solution, so that we can look for solutions in $H_0^1(\Om)$ rather than in spaces of classical (twice differentiable) solutions.

\subsection{Strong formulation}
\label{sec:poisson-weak:strong}

Let $m$ be a given source term (for example $m \in \Lp{\Om}$). The strong form of the forward problem is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:strong-forward}
  -\Delta u = m \quad \text{in } \Om,
  \qquad
  u = 0 \quad \text{on } \dom.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Derivation of the weak form}
\label{sec:poisson-weak:derivation}

We choose a test function in
\begin{equation}
  v \in H_0^1(\Om),
\end{equation}
which vanishes on $\dom$ in the trace sense. Multiplying \cref{eq:strong-forward} by $v$ and integrating over $\Om$ gives us
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:weak-derivation-step1}
  \int_{\Om} (-\Delta u)\, v \dx = \int_{\Om} m\, v \dx.
\end{equation}
The left-hand side contains second derivatives of $u$. To remove one of them, we recall Green's first identity~\citep[Lemma~5.23]{hc2026}:
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\Om} (-\Delta u)\, v \dx
  = \int_{\Om} \grad u \cdot \grad v \dx
  - \int_{\dom} \frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\, v \ds,
\end{equation}
where $n$ denotes the outward unit normal on $\dom$ and $\partial u / \partial n := \grad u \cdot n$ is the normal derivative of $u$. Because $v \in H_0^1(\Om)$ vanishes on $\dom$ (because of the boundary conditions), the boundary integral vanishes, and we obtain
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\Om} (-\Delta u)\, v \dx
  = \int_{\Om} \grad u \cdot \grad v \dx.
\end{equation}
Substituting this into \cref{eq:weak-derivation-step1} gives
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\Om} \grad u \cdot \grad v \dx
  = \int_{\Om} m\, v \dx,
\end{equation}
and this identity must hold for all test functions $v \in H_0^1(\Om)$. We collect the result into the following definition.

\begin{defn}[Weak formulation of the forward Poisson problem]
\label{defn:weak-poisson} Let $m \in \Lp{\Om}$. The \emph{weak formulation} of \cref{eq:strong-forward} is: find $u \in H_0^1(\Om)$ such that
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:weak-forward}
  \int_{\Om} \grad u \cdot \grad v \dx
  = \int_{\Om} m\, v \dx
  \qquad \forall v \in H_0^1(\Om).
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

It is also convenient to introduce the bilinear form
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:bilinear-b}
  b(u, v) := \int_{\Om} \grad u \cdot \grad v \dx
\end{equation}
and the linear functional
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:linear-ell}
  \ell(v) := \int_{\Om} m\, v \dx,
\end{equation}
so that \cref{eq:weak-forward} now reads
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:weak-forward-compact}
  \text{find } u \in H_0^1(\Om) \text{ such that }
  b(u, v) = \ell(v)
  \quad \forall v \in H_0^1(\Om).
\end{equation}
The bilinear form $b$ is continuous and, by the Poincar{\'e} inequality, coercive on $H_0^1(\Om)$. The linear functional $\ell$ is bounded on $H_0^1(\Om)$ whenever $m \in \Lp{\Om}$. The Lax-Milgram theorem then guarantees existence and uniqueness of the weak solution.

\section{The Poisson Optimal Control Problem}
\label{sec:poisson-optimisation}
% =============================================================================

Now, we have set up the forward problem and showed it is well posed, so each source term $m$ determines the state $u$. We now reverse the question. Rather than taking the source as given, we ask which source term $m$ makes the resulting state $u$ best match a given desired state $d$, while penalising large controls. In practice it is often this reversed direction, achieving a desired outcome rather than predicting one, that we care about. As an example, if we let $u$ be the steady-state temperature of a body whose boundary is held fixed and $m$ be a distributed heat source, we are asking which heating $m$ brings the temperature closest to a desired profile $d$ without expending excessive power. Problems of this shape, in which an input to a PDE is tuned to steer its solution towards a target, are very common in inverse problems and optimal design. The Poisson optimal control problem is a PDE-constrained optimisation problem of the form introduced in \cref{sec:prelim:problem}~\citep{shfp2017}.

Throughout the Poisson sections, we set
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:VM-spaces}
  V := H_0^1(\Om),
  \qquad
  \Mspace := \Lp{\Om}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Objective functional}
\label{sec:opt:objective}

Let $d \in \Lp{\Om}$ be a given desired state and let $\alpha > 0$.

\begin{defn}[Poisson objective functional]
\label{defn:objective} The \emph{objective functional} $J : V \times \Mspace \to \R$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:objective-J}
  J(u, m)
  := \tfrac{1}{2} \|u - d\|_{\Lp{\Om}}^2
  + \tfrac{\alpha}{2} \|m\|_{\Mspace}^2.
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

The first term measures the misfit between the state $u$ and the desired state $d$. The second term penalises large controls; the parameter $\alpha > 0$, called the \emph{Tikhonov} parameter, or equivalently the regularisation parameter, balances accuracy of fit against regularity of the control. Note that without the regularisation term the problem would be ill posed: the control-to-state map is smoothing, so many very different controls would produce nearly the same state and the misfit term alone fixes no unique, stable minimiser. The quadratic penalty makes the objective strictly convex and coercive in $m$, which ensures a unique minimiser that depends continuously on the data.

\subsection{The constrained optimisation problem}
\label{sec:opt:constrained}

Combining \cref{defn:objective} with the weak state equation \cref{eq:weak-forward-compact}, we get that the PDE-constrained optimisation problem reads as follows.

\begin{defn}[Poisson PDE-constrained optimisation problem]
\label{defn:full-problem} The \emph{Poisson PDE-constrained optimisation problem} is to find $(u, m) \in V \times \Mspace$ minimising
\begin{equation}
  \min_{(u, m) \in V \times \Mspace} J(u, m)
\end{equation}
subject to
\begin{equation}
  b(u, v) = \ell(v) \qquad \forall v \in V,
\end{equation}
where the bilinear form $b$ and the linear functional $\ell$ are those of \cref{eq:bilinear-b,eq:linear-ell}.
\end{defn}

\subsection{Reduced formulation}
\label{sec:opt:reduced}

The problem of \cref{defn:full-problem} is a \emph{constrained} minimisation: $u$ and $m$ are not independent, but are instead tied together by the state equation, and such problems in $V \times \Mspace$ are awkward to attack with the gradient-based methods that we will use in the rest of this dissertation. However, since the state equation determines $u$ uniquely from $m$, we have that the state is not a free unknown, and we can remove it, reducing the problem to an \emph{unconstrained} minimisation over the control alone~\citep{shfp2017}.

For each $m \in \Mspace$ there exists a unique state $u \in V$ satisfying the state equation, so we may view the state as a function of the control. To make this precise, we name the map that sends each control to its state as the solution operator: 

\begin{defn}[Poisson solution operator]
\label{defn:solution-operator} The \emph{solution operator} $S : \Mspace \to V$ assigns to each $m \in \Mspace$ the unique solution $S(m) := u(m) \in V$ of the weak state equation associated with $m$.
\end{defn}

Using the solution operator we eliminate the state from the optimisation problem.

\begin{defn}[Poisson reduced functional]
\label{defn:reduced-functional} The \emph{reduced functional} $\Jt : \Mspace \to \R$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
  \Jt(m) := J(S(m), m) = J(u(m), m).
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

The second equality is only a change of notation: $S(m)$ and $u(m)$ are two names for the same object, the unique state produced by the control $m$. The key step is that $\Jt$ is a function of $m$ alone, which is legitimate because the state equation determines that state uniquely, so that once $m$ is fixed no independent choice of $u$ remains.

We then have that the constrained problem of \cref{defn:full-problem} is equivalent to the unconstrained minimisation problem
\begin{equation}
  \min_{m \in \Mspace} \Jt(m).
\end{equation}

This reduced functional is the central object of the rest of this work -- it is what every optimiser we study minimises, its gradient (computed by the adjoint method of \cref{sec:poisson-derivative}) is what the Riesz map acts on, and its Hessian is where the mesh-dependence of \cref{sec:mesh-dependence} appears.

% =============================================================================
\section{Derivative of the Reduced Functional and the Adjoint Equation}
\label{sec:poisson-derivative}
% =============================================================================

We have now reduced the problem to the unconstrained minimisation of $\Jt$ over the control space. We want to do this minimisation with a gradient-based method, the natural choice for optimising over a function space, but these methods need the derivative of $\Jt$. Getting it by differentiating through the state equation would require a separate linearised solve for each control direction $\delta m$, whereas if we use the adjoint method, we can get the derivative in every direction from a single additional solve. It does this by introducing an auxiliary variable, the \emph{adjoint}, whose governing equation is chosen precisely so that the unknown state variation cancels from the expression for the derivative; the derivative in every direction can then be obtained from one adjoint solution. We first carry out this derivation for a general steady problem, and only then apply it to the Poisson problem. The argument is the same for any problem of this shape, so deriving it once in general lets us reuse it for the Burgers problem later. Throughout we use the chain rule (\cref{thm:chain}) and the calculus of partial Fr{\'e}chet derivatives (\cref{defn:partial-frechet}) introduced in \cref{sec:prelim:frechet}.

\subsection{The general derivation}
\label{sec:deriv:general}

We work with a general steady problem. The state $u$ lives in a Hilbert space $V$, the control $m$ lives in a Hilbert space $\Mspace$, and the objective is a functional $J : V \times \Mspace \to \R$. Each control $m$ determines a unique state $u(m) \in V$ through the state equation, and the reduced functional is $\Jt(m) = J(u(m), m)$ as before.

Applying the chain rule to $\Jt(m) = J(u(m), m)$ in the direction $\delta m \in \Mspace$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:chain-rule-applied}
  \mathrm{d}\Jt(m;\, \delta m)
  = \partial_u J(u, m;\, \mathrm{d} u_m(m;\, \delta m))
  + \partial_m J(u, m;\, \delta m),
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{d} u_m(m;\, \delta m)$ is the total derivative of the state with respect to the control in the direction $\delta m$ \citep[p.~32]{shfp2017}. Writing
\begin{equation}
  \delta u := \mathrm{d} u_m(m;\, \delta m) \in V,
\end{equation}
this becomes
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:chain-rule-shorter}
  \mathrm{d}\Jt(m;\, \delta m)
  = \partial_u J(u, m;\, \delta u)
  + \partial_m J(u, m;\, \delta m).
\end{equation}

This derivative involves two different objects: the objective $J$, through its partial derivatives, and the state equation, which is what determines the state variation $\delta u$. We now finish the derivation at this general level in three steps: we differentiate the constraint to get an equation for $\delta u$, we define an adjoint variable chosen precisely so that $\delta u$ cancels from the derivative, and we read off the result.

To differentiate the state equation, we first need to package it as a single object with the state and the control as explicit arguments. The following definition does exactly this.

\begin{defn}[Forward model operator]
\label{defn:forward-operator}
The \emph{forward model operator} $F(u, m;\, v)$ is the residual of the weak state equation, linear in the test function $v$, such that for each control $m$, the state $u(m) \in V$ is the unique solution of
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:state-general}
  F(u(m), m;\, v) = 0
  \qquad \forall v \in V
\end{equation}
\citep[p.~29]{shfp2017}.
\end{defn}

The first step is to obtain the equation that determines $\delta u$, which we get by differentiating the state equation. \Cref{eq:state-general} holds for every control $m$, and its left-hand side depends on $m$ both directly and through the state $u(m)$, so differentiating in the direction $\delta m$ gives the following equation.

\begin{defn}[Tangent linear equation]
\label{defn:tlm}
The \emph{tangent linear equation}, also known as the tangent linear model \citep[p.~32]{shfp2017}, is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:tlm-implicit}
  \partial_u F(u, m;\, \delta u, v)
  + \partial_m F(u, m;\, \delta m, v) = 0
  \qquad \forall v \in V.
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

Once the partial derivatives of $F$ are known, this is a linear equation determining $\delta u$ from $\delta m$.

The only obstacle to evaluating $\mathrm{d}\Jt$ cheaply is the term $\partial_u J(u, m;\, \delta u)$ in the chain rule, which drags in the direction-specific state variation $\delta u$. The adjoint is defined precisely to remove it.

\begin{defn}[Adjoint variable]
\label{defn:adjoint}
The \emph{adjoint variable} $\lambda \in V$ is the unique solution of
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:adjoint-general}
  \partial_u F(u, m;\, w, \lambda)
  = \partial_u J(u, m;\, w)
  \qquad \forall w \in V.
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

Note where $\lambda$ is in this equation. It occupies the test-function slot of $\partial_u F$, while the direction slot is filled by the arbitrary $w$. Compared with the tangent linear equation, the roles of the direction and the test function are swapped, and this swap is what the word \emph{adjoint} refers to.

The adjoint is exactly what we need to remove $\delta u$ from the chain rule. Choosing the direction $w = \delta u$ in the adjoint equation gives
\begin{equation}
  \partial_u J(u, m;\, \delta u) = \partial_u F(u, m;\, \delta u, \lambda),
\end{equation}
and choosing the test function $v = \lambda$ in the tangent linear equation \cref{eq:tlm-implicit} gives
\begin{equation}
  \partial_u F(u, m;\, \delta u, \lambda) = -\,\partial_m F(u, m;\, \delta m, \lambda).
\end{equation}
Substituting these into \cref{eq:chain-rule-shorter}, the state variation $\delta u$ disappears completely:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dJtilde-general}
  \mathrm{d}\Jt(m;\, \delta m)
  = \partial_m J(u, m;\, \delta m)
  - \partial_m F(u, m;\, \delta m, \lambda)
  \qquad \forall \delta m \in \Mspace.
\end{equation}
So now, the right-hand side only involves the control direction $\delta m$ and the single adjoint solution $\lambda$, and hence one adjoint solve gives the derivative of $\Jt$ in every direction.

One step of the recipe remains. The derivative $\mathrm{d}\Jt(m)$ is an element of the dual space $\Mspace^{*}$, but a gradient-based optimiser steps along directions in $\Mspace$ itself, so we use the Riesz map of \cref{defn:rieszmap} to convert the former into the latter. Which inner product the Riesz map belongs to is a genuine choice, and the consequences of that choice are the subject of \cref{sec:mesh-dependence} -- every gradient in this dissertation, whether $\ell^{2}$, $L^{2}$ or $H^{1}$, is an instance of the following definition.

\begin{defn}[$H$-gradient]
\label{defn:Hgradient}
Let $H$ be a choice of inner product on $\Mspace$, with Riesz map $\Rmap_H$. The \emph{$H$-gradient} of $\Jt$ at $m$ is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Hgradient}
  \grad_{H} \Jt(m) := \Rmap_H(\mathrm{d}\Jt(m)) \in \Mspace.
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

This completes the general derivation. For a concrete problem, we compute the four partial derivatives, solve the adjoint equation, read the derivative off \cref{eq:dJtilde-general}, and apply the Riesz map of our chosen inner product.

\subsection{The Poisson tangent linear equation}
\label{sec:deriv:tangent}

We now apply the general derivation to the Poisson problem. Following the recipe above, all we have to do is compute the four partial derivatives, starting with those of the forward model operator, which we first use (\cref{defn:forward-operator}) in the Poisson problem.

\begin{defn}[Poisson forward model operator]
\label{defn:poisson-forward-operator} The \emph{forward model operator} of the Poisson problem is defined as the residual of the weak state equation:
\begin{equation}
  F(u, m;\, v) := b(u, v) - \inner{m}{v}{\Lp{\Om}}.
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

The weak state equation \cref{eq:weak-forward-compact} is then exactly $F(u(m), m;\, v) = 0$ for all $v \in V$, so the Poisson problem fits the general setting. We compute the two partial derivatives of the forward model operator $F$. First, we want the derivative with respect to $u$. For $\varepsilon \in \R$, set $u_\varepsilon := u + \varepsilon \delta u$. Using linearity of $b$ in its first argument,
\begin{equation}
  F(u_\varepsilon, m;\, v)
  = b(u, v) - \inner{m}{v}{\Lp{\Om}}
  + \varepsilon\, b(\delta u, v).
\end{equation}
The corresponding difference quotient is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{F(u + \varepsilon \delta u, m;\, v) - F(u, m;\, v)}{\varepsilon}
  = b(\delta u, v),
\end{equation}
and taking $\varepsilon \to 0$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:partial-Fu}
  \partial_u F(u, m;\, \delta u, v) = b(\delta u, v).
\end{equation}

Next, the derivative with respect to $m$. By the analogous argument, perturbing $m$ in the direction $\delta m$, we get
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:partial-Fm}
  \partial_m F(u, m;\, \delta m, v)
  = -\inner{\delta m}{v}{\Lp{\Om}}.
\end{equation}

Substituting \cref{eq:partial-Fu,eq:partial-Fm} into the general tangent linear equation \cref{eq:tlm-implicit} gives its explicit form for the Poisson problem.

\begin{prop}[Poisson tangent linear equation]
\label{prop:tangent-linear} The state variation $\delta u \in V$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:tangent-linear}
  b(\delta u, v) = \inner{\delta m}{v}{\Lp{\Om}}
  \qquad \forall v \in V.
\end{equation}
\end{prop}

Notice that the tangent linear equation \cref{eq:tangent-linear} has exactly the same form as the forward weak problem \cref{eq:weak-forward-compact}, with $\delta u$ and $\delta m$ in place of $u$ and $m$. This is because the Poisson problem is linear, and a linear problem is its own tangent linear model.

\subsection{Partial derivatives of the Poisson objective functional}
\label{sec:deriv:Jpartial}

Now we compute the partial derivatives of the objective functional $J$. First, the derivative of $J$ with respect to $u$. Setting $u_\varepsilon := u + \varepsilon v$ and expanding, we get
\begin{equation}
  J(u_\varepsilon, m)
  = J(u, m)
  + \varepsilon \inner{u - d}{v}{\Lp{\Om}}
  + \tfrac{\varepsilon^2}{2} \|v\|_{\Lp{\Om}}^2,
\end{equation}
and so
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:partial-Ju}
  \partial_u J(u, m;\, v) = \inner{u - d}{v}{\Lp{\Om}}.
\end{equation}

Next, the derivative of $J$ with respect to $m$. Setting $m_\varepsilon := m + \varepsilon \delta m$ and expanding $\|m + \varepsilon \delta m\|^2$,
\begin{equation}
  J(u, m_\varepsilon)
  = J(u, m)
  + \alpha \varepsilon \inner{m}{\delta m}{\Lp{\Om}}
  + \tfrac{\alpha \varepsilon^2}{2} \|\delta m\|_{\Lp{\Om}}^2,
\end{equation}
giving
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:partial-Jm}
  \partial_m J(u, m;\, \delta m) = \alpha \inner{m}{\delta m}{\Lp{\Om}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{The Poisson adjoint problem}
\label{sec:deriv:adjoint}

The adjoint equation reverses the direction of the state equation. Where the state equation maps the control forward to the state, the adjoint equation propagates the sensitivity of the objective backward, to the control \citep[p.~33]{shfp2017}. Substituting the Poisson partial derivatives \cref{eq:partial-Fu,eq:partial-Ju} into the general definition of the adjoint variable (\cref{defn:adjoint}) gives $b(w, \lambda) = \inner{u - d}{w}{\Lp{\Om}}$ for all $w \in V$. Since $b$ is symmetric (we have $b(u, v) = b(v, u)$ because $\grad u \cdot \grad v = \grad v \cdot \grad u$), this gives the adjoint equation for the Poisson problem in explicit form.

\begin{prop}[Poisson adjoint equation]
\label{prop:adjoint} The adjoint variable $\lambda \in V$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:adjoint-implicit}
  b(\lambda, v) = \inner{u - d}{v}{\Lp{\Om}}
  \qquad \forall v \in V.
\end{equation}
\end{prop}

The \emph{adjoint problem} is to find the $\lambda \in V$ that satisfies this adjoint equation. It is a single Poisson-type solve, with the same bilinear form $b$ as the forward problem of \cref{defn:weak-poisson}, but driven by the misfit $u - d$ in place of the control $m$. The bilinear form $b$ is continuous and coercive on $V$, and the right-hand side is a continuous linear functional on $V$, so existence and uniqueness of the adjoint follow from Lax-Milgram \citep[p.~14]{shfp2017}. Its one solution $\lambda$ is all we need to evaluate the derivative of $\Jt$ in every direction, which is what we do next.

\subsection{Derivative of the Poisson reduced functional}
\label{sec:deriv:reduced} 

With all four partial derivatives computed, the derivative of $\Jt$ falls straight out of the general formula \cref{eq:dJtilde-general}. Substituting the Poisson partials $\partial_m J(u, m;\, \delta m) = \alpha \inner{m}{\delta m}{\Lp{\Om}}$ and $\partial_m F(u, m;\, \delta m, \lambda) = -\inner{\delta m}{\lambda}{\Lp{\Om}}$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dJtilde-pre-riesz}
  \mathrm{d}\Jt(m;\, \delta m)
  = \inner{\lambda}{\delta m}{\Lp{\Om}}
  + \alpha \inner{m}{\delta m}{\Lp{\Om}}
  \qquad \forall \delta m \in \Mspace,
\end{equation}
where $u = u(m)$ is the state corresponding to $m$ and $\lambda$ is the adjoint solution.

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$L^2$}{L2}-gradient}
\label{sec:deriv:l2grad}

We now have the derivative $\mathrm{d}\Jt$, but a gradient-based method cannot use it as it stands. These methods improve the control by stepping along a search direction, so they need an actual element of the control space $\Mspace$ to add to the current control $m$, whereas the derivative is a functional that acts on directions rather than being one itself. So, we need to convert it into a genuine direction in $\Mspace$, and it is in this conversion that the choice of inner product matters.

The reduced derivative \cref{eq:dJtilde-pre-riesz} is a continuous linear functional acting on directions $\delta m \in \Mspace$, which means that it is an element of the dual space $\Mspace^{*}$. By the Riesz representation theorem (\cref{thm:riesz}), there exists a unique element of $\Mspace$ representing it. The following is the $H$-gradient of \cref{defn:Hgradient}, with $H$ taken to be the $L^{2}$ inner product.

\begin{defn}[$L^2$-gradient]
\label{defn:l2gradient} Let $\Rmap_{L^2} : \Mspace^{*} \to \Mspace$ denote the Riesz map associated with the $\Lp{\Om}$ inner product. The \emph{$L^2$-gradient} of $\Jt$ at $m$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
  \grad \Jt(m) := \Rmap_{L^2}(\mathrm{d}\Jt(m)) \in \Mspace,
\end{equation}
that is, $\grad \Jt(m)$ is the unique element of $\Mspace$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{d}\Jt(m;\, \delta m)
  = \inner{\grad \Jt(m)}{\delta m}{\Lp{\Om}}
  \qquad \forall \delta m \in \Mspace.
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

Linearity of the inner product lets us factor \cref{eq:dJtilde-pre-riesz} as
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{d}\Jt(m;\, \delta m)
  = \inner{\lambda + \alpha m}{\delta m}{\Lp{\Om}}
  \qquad \forall \delta m \in \Mspace.
\end{equation}
Comparing with the definition of $\grad \Jt$ gives us that
\begin{equation}
    \grad \Jt(m) = \lambda + \alpha m.
  \label{eq:l2gradient-poisson}
\end{equation}

This completes the gradient derivation for the Poisson problem. A single forward solve for the state $u$ and a single adjoint solve for $\lambda$ combine to give $\grad \Jt(m) = \lambda + \alpha m$, the direction a gradient-based optimiser uses at each step.

\section{Numerical Implementation and Verification of the Poisson Problem}
\label{sec:poisson-numerics}
% =============================================================================

Having derived the weak form of the forward problem, the adjoint equation and the $L^2$-gradient of the reduced functional, we now implement the Poisson optimal control problem numerically. The implementation serves two purposes: to verify that the finite element discretisation of the forward problem behaves as expected, and to confirm that the adjoint-based gradient computation is correct before extending the same approach to more complicated problems in \cref{sec:burgers-numerics,sec:mesh-dependence}.

We carry out the numerical work in two stages. First, we implement the forward Poisson problem in plain Python using a simple $P_1$ finite element method on a triangular mesh; the hand-written implementation makes the main finite element steps explicit, namely mesh construction, basis functions, element-wise assembly and the imposition of homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. Second, we implement the same forward problem in Firedrake~\citep{firedrake2016} together with the adjoint solve, the gradient computation and the Taylor test. The two implementations share the same mathematical content; the first makes the finite element method explicit, and the second expresses it more compactly using a domain-specific language.

\subsection{Forward Poisson by hand}
\label{sec:poisson-numerics:byhand}

We start from scratch, without a finite element library, so that nothing about the method is hidden. Implementing the forward solve by hand puts every step (the mesh, the basis functions, the assembly and the boundary conditions) in plain view, where each can be checked directly against the weak form of \cref{sec:poisson-weak}.

Before describing that implementation, we define three finite element notions on which it rests: the mesh it is built on, the degrees of freedom it solves for, and the numerical quadrature it uses to evaluate the element integrals.

\begin{defn}[Mesh]
\label{defn:mesh}
A \emph{mesh}, or \emph{triangulation}, $\mathcal{T}_h$ of a polygonal domain $\Om$ is a finite set of triangles $\{K_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$ such that the interiors of distinct triangles are disjoint, the union of the triangles is the closure of $\Om$, and no vertex of one triangle lies in the interior of an edge of another \citep[Defn.~1.2]{hc2026}. The last condition makes the mesh \emph{conforming}, meaning that it has no hanging nodes. Each $K \in \mathcal{T}_h$ is a \emph{cell}, or equivalently an \emph{element}, and we write $h_K$ for its diameter and
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:mesh-size}
  h := \max_{K \in \mathcal{T}_h} h_K
\end{equation}
for the \emph{mesh size}. A mesh is \emph{uniform} when all of its cells have the same diameter, and \emph{non-uniform}, or \emph{graded}, when they do not.
\end{defn}

\begin{defn}[Degree of freedom]
\label{defn:dof}
A \emph{degree of freedom} is one of the unknowns in the finite element system, namely the coefficient multiplying a single basis function. For the $P_1$ Lagrange space used here, there is one degree of freedom per mesh vertex.
\end{defn}

\begin{defn}[Numerical quadrature]
\label{defn:quadrature}
A \emph{quadrature rule} approximates the integral of a function $f$ over an element $K$ by a weighted sum of its values at finitely many points,
\begin{equation}
  \int_K f \dx \approx \sum_{q} w_q\, f(x_q),
\end{equation}
where the $x_q$ are the \emph{quadrature points} and the $w_q$ the corresponding \emph{weights}. The rule has \emph{degree} $p$ if this approximation is exact whenever $f$ is a polynomial of degree at most $p$.
\end{defn}

We implement the forward problem in plain Python using a continuous piecewise linear finite element method. The domain $\Om = (0, 1)^2$ is triangulated using a structured mesh obtained by splitting each square cell into two triangles (\cref{fig:poisson-mesh}), and the discrete space is the standard $P_1$ Lagrange space.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/poisson_meshes.pdf}
  \caption{The structured triangulation of the unit square $\Om = (0,1)^2$ we used for the forward Poisson problem, shown at three refinement levels $n_x = 4, 8, 16$. Each square cell is split into two triangles along its bottom-left to top-right diagonal, giving $2 n_x^2$ elements and $(n_x + 1)^2$ vertices. The convergence study refines this mesh uniformly up to $n_x = 512$. The hand-written implementation and Firedrake's \texttt{UnitSquareMesh} produce this identical mesh.}
  \label{fig:poisson-mesh}
\end{figure}

% For each element, the implementation assembles the local stiffness matrix and the local load vector by mapping from the physical triangle to a reference triangle and applying numerical quadrature. The local contributions are then accumulated into a global sparse linear system. Since the problem is subject to homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, the algebraic system is modified so that boundary degrees of freedom are fixed to zero. These steps are summarised in \cref{alg:poisson-assembly}.

We assemble the global linear system one element at a time. For each triangle, we first map it onto a reference triangle and apply numerical quadrature (\cref{defn:quadrature}), which gives us a local stiffness matrix and a local load vector. We then add these local contributions into a single global sparse system. Finally, since the problem has homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, we modify the assembled system so that the boundary degrees of freedom (\cref{defn:dof}) are fixed to zero. These steps are summarised in \cref{alg:poisson-assembly}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Assembly and solution of the $P_1$ forward Poisson system, as carried out by hand.}
\label{alg:poisson-assembly}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State construct the structured triangular mesh of $\Om$
\State initialise the global stiffness matrix $A \gets 0$ and load vector $b \gets 0$
\ForAll{triangles $K$ with vertices $x_0, x_1, x_2$}
  \State form the affine map to the reference triangle, $J \gets [\,x_1 - x_0 \;\; x_2 - x_0\,]$
  \State $\operatorname{area}(K) \gets \tfrac{1}{2}\lvert\det J\rvert$
  \State compute the physical basis gradients $\grad \phi_i \gets J^{-\top}\, \grad_{\!\xi}\, \hat{\phi}_i$
  \State local stiffness $A^K_{ij} \gets \operatorname{area}(K)\; \grad \phi_i \cdot \grad \phi_j$
  \State local load $b^K_i \gets \sum_q w_q\, \lvert\det J\rvert\, m(x_q)\, \hat{\phi}_i(\xi_q)$ \Comment{quadrature}
  \State add $A^K$ and $b^K$ into the global $A$ and $b$
\EndFor
\ForAll{boundary vertices $p$} \Comment{homogeneous Dirichlet condition}
  \State overwrite row $p$ of $A$ with the identity row and set $b_p \gets 0$
\EndFor
\State solve the linear system $A u = b$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The hand-written implementation focuses on clarity rather than efficiency -- writing the assembly explicitly makes the connection between the weak form derived in \cref{sec:poisson-weak} and the finite element matrices that appear in the computation transparent.

\subsection{Forward Poisson solve in Firedrake}
\label{sec:poisson-numerics:firedrake}

The hand-written solve is transparent, but it only does the forward problem. Everything that follows, the adjoint solve, the gradient, and the mesh-dependence experiments, relies on the algorithmic differentiation that Firedrake and its pyadjoint extension provide, so we now want to implement the same forward problem there. This also gives an independent check, since two different codes should now agree on the same discretisation.

So, we now implement the same forward problem in Firedrake~\citep{firedrake2016} using continuous Galerkin elements of degree one. The variational forms are written directly from the weak formulation \cref{eq:weak-forward-compact}, and the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition is imposed strongly on $\dom$. The complete forward solve is shown in \cref{lst:poisson-firedrake}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
V = FunctionSpace(mesh, "CG", 1)
u = TrialFunction(V)
v = TestFunction(V)

a = inner(grad(u), grad(v)) * dx
L = m * v * dx
bc = DirichletBC(V, 0.0, "on_boundary")

u_h = Function(V)
solve(a == L, u_h, bcs=bc)
\end{lstlisting}
\caption{The forward Poisson solve in Firedrake. The bilinear form \texttt{a} and linear form \texttt{L} are transcribed almost verbatim from the weak formulation \cref{eq:weak-forward-compact}, with \texttt{m} the source term interpolated onto the finite element space.}
\label{lst:poisson-firedrake}
\end{figure}

The Firedrake implementation is notably more concise than the plain Python version, but represents the same finite element problem. Working in Firedrake also lets us express the forward solve, the adjoint solve and the gradient computation in a form that remains close to the mathematical notation introduced earlier in the work; this is critical for the adjoint-based experiments of \cref{sec:mesh-dependence}, which rely on the algorithmic differentiation provided by the pyadjoint extension to Firedrake~\citep{mitusch2019}.

\subsection{Manufactured solution and convergence study}
\label{sec:poisson-numerics:mms}

To test the correctness of the forward implementations, we use the method of manufactured solutions, following the presentation in Sect.~6.3 of \citet{hc2026}. We prescribe a smooth function satisfying the homogeneous boundary conditions and choose the source term accordingly. We define
\begin{equation}
  u_{\mathrm{exact}}(x, y) = \sin(\pi x) \sin(\pi y),
\end{equation}
which vanishes on $\dom$. The corresponding source term is
\begin{equation}
  m(x, y) = -\Delta u_{\mathrm{exact}}(x, y)
         = 2\pi^{2} \sin(\pi x) \sin(\pi y).
\end{equation}
With this choice, the continuous problem admits the known exact solution $u = u_{\mathrm{exact}}$.

Let $u_h$ denote the finite element approximation on a mesh of size $h$. For sufficiently smooth solutions, standard finite element theory predicts that the $\Lp{\Om}$ error for piecewise linear elements satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \|u - u_h\|_{\Lp{\Om}} = \mathcal{O}(h^{2}).
\end{equation}
We estimate the experimental order of convergence between two successive mesh levels by
\begin{equation}
  q = \frac{\log(e_H / e_h)}{\log(h_H / h_h)},
\end{equation}
where $e_h = \|u - u_h\|_{\Lp{\Om}}$.

\subsection{Adjoint solve and gradient computation}
\label{sec:poisson-numerics:adjoint}

We now implement the gradient in Firedrake. This is the step that matters most for the rest of the work, as every optimiser we will later study uses this gradient at each iteration, so it must be computed correctly.

The derivation of \cref{sec:poisson-derivative} states that the gradient of the reduced functional is computed by first solving the adjoint equation and then forming the $L^2$-gradient $\grad \Jt(m) = \lambda + \alpha m$. The Firedrake implementation does this in three stages:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item solve the forward Poisson problem to obtain the state $u$;
  \item solve the adjoint problem to obtain $\lambda$;
  \item compute the gradient as the function $\lambda + \alpha m$.
\end{enumerate}

The numerical implementation mirrors the theoretical derivation. In particular, it exploits the fact that the adjoint equation lets us compute the derivative of the reduced functional without solving a separate tangent linear problem for every perturbation direction.

\subsection{Taylor test for gradient verification}
\label{sec:poisson-numerics:taylor}

After implementing the adjoint and gradient in Firedrake, we carry out a Taylor test to verify that the computed gradient is correct. The Taylor test is a standard practical check for adjoint implementations~\citep{mitusch2019}.

Let $\delta m$ be a perturbation direction and let $h > 0$ be a small parameter. The reduced functional satisfies the Taylor expansion
\begin{equation}
  \Jt(m + h \delta m)
  = \Jt(m) + h\, \mathrm{d}\Jt(m;\, \delta m) + \mathcal{O}(h^{2}).
\end{equation}
If the gradient implementation is correct, the second-order remainder
\begin{equation}
  R_2(h)
  := \left|
       \Jt(m + h \delta m) - \Jt(m) - h\, \mathrm{d}\Jt(m;\, \delta m)
     \right|
\end{equation}
decays as $\mathcal{O}(h^{2})$ as $h \to 0$.

Using a smooth perturbation direction, we obtained the remainder sequence reported in \cref{tab:poisson-taylor}.

\section{The Forward Problem for the Scalar Viscous Burgers Equation}
\label{sec:burgers-weak}
% =============================================================================

Having done the full programme (forward solve, adjoint equation, reduced gradient, and numerical verification) through for the linear Poisson problem, we now repeat it for a harder, nonlinear model problem: the scalar viscous Burgers equation. Following the notation established earlier, we write the state variable as $u$ and the control as $m$. This is a scalar simplification of the time-dependent viscous Burgers model considered by \citet{shfp2017}, whose treatment is vector-valued. The main new features compared with the Poisson problem are nonlinearity in $u$ (through the convection term), time dependence (which we deal with using backward Euler in \cref{sec:burgers-numerics}), and a backward-in-time adjoint solve.

\subsection{Strong formulation}
\label{sec:burgers-weak:strong}

Let $\Om \subset \R$ be a bounded spatial domain and let $T > 0$. The scalar viscous Burgers equation reads
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-strong}
  \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}
  + u \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}
  - \nu \frac{\partial^{2} u}{\partial x^{2}} = m
  \qquad \text{in } (0, T) \times \Om,
\end{equation}
subject to homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions
\begin{equation}
  u = 0 \qquad \text{on } (0, T) \times \dom,
\end{equation}
and initial condition
\begin{equation}
  u(0, x) = u_0(x) \qquad \text{for } x \in \Om.
\end{equation}
Here, $\nu > 0$ is the viscosity parameter.

\subsection{Weak formulation}
\label{sec:burgers-weak:weak}

For each fixed $t \in (0, T)$, we take the spatial state space to be $V := H_0^1(\Om)$. Let $v \in V$ be a test function. Multiplying \cref{eq:burgers-strong} by $v$ and integrating over $\Om$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\Om} \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\, v \dx
  + \int_{\Om} u \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\, v \dx
  - \nu \int_{\Om} \frac{\partial^{2} u}{\partial x^{2}}\, v \dx
  = \int_{\Om} m v \dx.
\end{equation}
Integrating the diffusion term by parts and using $v = 0$ on $\dom$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-weak}
  \int_{\Om} \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\, v \dx
  + \int_{\Om} u \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\, v \dx
  + \nu \int_{\Om} \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\,
                   \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} \dx
  = \int_{\Om} m v \dx
  \qquad \forall v \in V,
\end{equation}
for almost every $t \in (0, T)$.

The convection term $u\,\partial_x u$ makes the Burgers dynamics qualitatively different from the linear Poisson problem. The local wave speed is the solution value $u$ itself, so taller parts of the profile travel faster than shorter ones. This carries the profile forward, and the faster-moving regions of the solution catch up with the slower regions ahead of them, so the front of the wave steepens. The viscosity $\nu$ prevents this front from becoming a true discontinuity, and it also slowly damps the amplitude. \Cref{fig:burgers-evolution} shows this behaviour for a representative forward solve. This nonlinear steepening is one of the two main new features of the Burgers case; the other is the time dependence, which is what makes the adjoint run backward in time (\cref{sec:burgers-derivative}).

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{figures/burgers_evolution.pdf}
  \caption{A representative solution of the forward Burgers equation with viscosity $\nu = 0.05$, starting from $u_0(x) = \sin(\pi x)$ with no forcing, shown at successive times.}
  \label{fig:burgers-evolution}
\end{figure}

\section{Derivative of the Reduced Functional for the Burgers Problem}
\label{sec:burgers-derivative}
% =============================================================================

With the forward Burgers problem of \cref{sec:burgers-weak} now in place, we extend the adjoint-based derivative analysis of \cref{sec:poisson-derivative} to the scalar Burgers problem. Here we have to be careful about which problem we differentiate. In practice, we never solve a time-dependent problem over the whole of space-time at once -- doing so would turn a problem in $d$ space dimensions into one in $d + 1$ dimensions, which is far too expensive. Instead, the standard approach, and the one our implementation takes (\cref{sec:burgers-numerics}), is to discretise in time first; we use the backward Euler method. The forward problem then becomes a \emph{sequence} of spatial problems, one per time step, solved in turn, and the functional we minimise is a functional of these time-discrete states. We therefore differentiate this time-discrete problem directly, so that the derivation matches what we actually compute. The forward map is a composition of time steps, so we differentiate it using the chain rule together with the implicit function theorem, applying the general derivation of \cref{sec:deriv:general} one step at a time, while keeping space continuous throughout. The spatial discretisation then proceeds exactly as for the Poisson problem. The adjoint of the time-continuous problem still appears, but only at the end, as the limit of the discrete adjoint as $\Delta t \to 0$.

\subsection{Time discretisation and the composed forward problem}
\label{sec:burgers-deriv:setup}

Let $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_N = T$ be a uniform grid on $[0, T]$ with step $\Delta t = T / N$, and write $u^n \in V$ for the approximation to $u(t_n, \cdot)$, with $V := H_0^1(\Om)$ as before and the initial state $u^0 = u_0$ is given and fixed. The backward Euler discretisation of the weak form \cref{eq:burgers-weak} replaces the time derivative by a difference quotient, and evaluates every other term at the new time level. Given $u^n$, the next state $u^{n+1} \in V$ solves $F^{n+1}(u^{n+1}, u^n, m^{n+1};\, v) = 0$ for all $v \in V$, where
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-step}
  F^{n+1}(u^{n+1}, u^n, m^{n+1};\, v)
  := \int_{\Om}
     \frac{u^{n+1} - u^n}{\Delta t}\, v
     + u^{n+1} \frac{\partial u^{n+1}}{\partial x}\, v
     + \nu \frac{\partial u^{n+1}}{\partial x} \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}
     - m^{n+1}\, v
     \dx,
\end{equation}
and $m^{n+1} \in \Lp{\Om}$ is the control at the new time level. This is one residual per time step, each of exactly the form assumed in the general setting of \cref{sec:deriv:general}, except that every residual now involves \emph{two} states: the one being solved for, $u^{n+1}$, and the already-known previous state $u^n$.

Each step is a nonlinear spatial problem (solved by Newton's method in \cref{sec:burgers-numerics}), and by the implicit function theorem it defines the new state as a differentiable function of the data it depends on: $u^{n+1} = S^{n+1}(u^n, m^{n+1})$. Chaining the steps together, the forward map is a composition moving forwards in time,
\begin{equation}
  u^1 = S^1(u^0, m^1), \qquad
  u^2 = S^2(u^1, m^2), \qquad \ldots, \qquad
  u^N = S^N(u^{N-1}, m^N),
\end{equation}
so each state $u^n$ depends on the controls $m^1, \ldots, m^n$ up to its own time level and on nothing later.

The control is the collection $m = (m^1, \ldots, m^N)$, living in the product space $\Mspace_{\Delta t} := \Lp{\Om}^N$, and the objective is the time-discrete tracking functional
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-objective}
  J(u, m)
  := \Delta t \sum_{n=1}^{N}
     \left(
       \tfrac{1}{2} \|u^n - d^n\|_{\Lp{\Om}}^2
       + \tfrac{\alpha}{2} \|m^n\|_{\Lp{\Om}}^2
     \right),
  \qquad d^n := d(t_n, \cdot),
\end{equation}
which is the backward Euler quadrature of the space--time tracking objective, and the objective the implementation of \cref{sec:burgers-numerics} evaluates. On $\Mspace_{\Delta t}$ we use the matching time-discrete $L^2$ inner product
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-control-ip}
  \inner{m}{m'}{\Delta t}
  := \Delta t \sum_{n=1}^{N} \inner{m^n}{m'^n}{\Lp{\Om}}.
\end{equation}
The reduced functional is $\Jt(m) := J(u(m), m)$ as before, where $u(m) = (u^1(m), \ldots, u^N(m))$ is the sequence of states produced by the composition above.

The derivation now follows the same three steps as the general case: differentiate each constraint to obtain a tangent linear system, introduce one adjoint variable per constraint, chosen so that every state variation cancels at once, and read the gradient off the result. The two new features are the nonlinearity of the convection term, which we have to linearise, and the coupling between time levels, which is what makes the adjoint run backward in time.

\subsection{The tangent linear system}
\label{sec:burgers-deriv:tlm}

As in the Poisson case (\cref{sec:deriv:tangent}), the first step is to write the state variations in terms of the control perturbation by differentiating the constraints, of which there are now $N$, one per time step. Let $\delta m = (\delta m^1, \ldots, \delta m^N)$ be a control perturbation and let $\delta u^n$ denote the resulting variation in $u^n$. Since the initial state is fixed, $\delta u^0 = 0$. Each constraint
\begin{equation}
  F^{n+1}\bigl(u^{n+1}(m),\, u^n(m),\, m^{n+1};\, v\bigr) = 0
\end{equation}
holds identically in $m$, so differentiating in the direction $\delta m$ gives, for $n = 0, \ldots, N - 1$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-tlm}
  \partial_{u^{n+1}} F^{n+1}(\delta u^{n+1}, v)
  + \partial_{u^n} F^{n+1}(\delta u^n, v)
  + \partial_{m^{n+1}} F^{n+1}(\delta m^{n+1}, v)
  = 0
  \qquad \forall v \in V,
\end{equation}
where we omit from the notation the point $(u^{n+1}, u^n, m^{n+1})$ at which the partial derivatives are evaluated, to keep the formulas short; each derivative is still taken at these states. Each residual has three arguments, so there are three partial derivatives per step, and the only nonlinear term is the convection term $u^{n+1} \frac{\partial u^{n+1}}{\partial x}$ of \cref{eq:burgers-step}. The derivative of the nonlinear convection term is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-conv-lin}
  (u + \varepsilon \delta u)
  \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(u + \varepsilon \delta u)
  = u \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}
  + \varepsilon
    \left(
      \delta u \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}
      + u \frac{\partial \delta u}{\partial x}
    \right)
  + \mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).
\end{equation}
We now substitute $u^{n+1} + \varepsilon w$ for $u^{n+1}$ in the step residual \cref{eq:burgers-step}. The time and diffusion terms are linear in $u^{n+1}$, the source term does not contain it, and the convection term expands according to \cref{eq:burgers-conv-lin}, applied with $u = u^{n+1}$ and $\delta u = w$. Collecting the terms by powers of $\varepsilon$ gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  F^{n+1}(u^{n+1} + \varepsilon w,\, u^n,\, m^{n+1};\, v)
  &= F^{n+1}(u^{n+1}, u^n, m^{n+1};\, v) \\
  &\quad + \varepsilon \int_{\Om}
    \frac{w\, v}{\Delta t}
    + \left( w \frac{\partial u^{n+1}}{\partial x}
             + u^{n+1} \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} \right) v
    + \nu \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}
    \dx
  + \mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
As in the Poisson case, the difference quotient converges as $\varepsilon \to 0$ to the $\varepsilon$-coefficient, so the derivative of the step residual with respect to $u^{n+1}$ in a direction $w \in V$ is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-dFdunew}
  \partial_{u^{n+1}} F^{n+1}(w, v)
  = \int_{\Om}
    \frac{w\, v}{\Delta t}
    + \left( w \frac{\partial u^{n+1}}{\partial x}
             + u^{n+1} \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} \right) v
    + \nu \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}
    \dx,
\end{equation}
while the previous state and the control only enter the residual linearly, giving
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-dFduold}
  \partial_{u^n} F^{n+1}(w, v) = -\int_{\Om} \frac{w\, v}{\Delta t} \dx,
  \qquad
  \partial_{m^{n+1}} F^{n+1}(\delta m^{n+1}, v) = -\int_{\Om} \delta m^{n+1}\, v \dx.
\end{equation}
Starting from $\delta u^0 = 0$, the system \cref{eq:burgers-tlm} is therefore a forward sweep, where each step is a \emph{linear} spatial problem determining $\delta u^{n+1}$ from $\delta u^n$ and $\delta m^{n+1}$. Evaluating the derivative of $\Jt$ this way would cost one such sweep for every control direction $\delta m$, which is exactly the cost the adjoint avoids.

\subsection{The adjoint system}
\label{sec:burgers-deriv:adjoint}

As in the Poisson case (\cref{sec:deriv:adjoint}), we introduce adjoint variables to remove the state variations from the derivative, but now there are $N$ of them, one per constraint. The chain rule, applied in each of the $2N$ arguments of the objective \cref{eq:burgers-objective}, gives
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{d}\Jt(m;\, \delta m)
  = \sum_{k=1}^{N}
    \left(
      \partial_{u^k} J(\delta u^k)
      + \partial_{m^k} J(\delta m^k)
    \right),
\end{equation}
with the partial derivatives computed exactly as in the Poisson case (\cref{sec:deriv:Jpartial}): only the $k$-th summand of \cref{eq:burgers-objective} contains $u^k$ or $m^k$, and differentiating that summand gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-Jpartials}
  \partial_{u^k} J(w) = \Delta t \inner{u^k - d^k}{w}{\Lp{\Om}},
  \qquad
  \partial_{m^k} J(\delta m^k)
  = \alpha\, \Delta t \inner{m^k}{\delta m^k}{\Lp{\Om}},
\end{equation}
and the terms that we have to be aware of are again the ones containing the state variations $\delta u^k$. We define the adjoint variables so that all of them cancel at once.

\begin{defn}[Discrete Burgers adjoint variables]
\label{defn:burgers-adjoint}
The adjoint variables $\lambda^N, \lambda^{N-1}, \ldots, \lambda^1 \in V$ are the solutions, in this order, of
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-adjoint-def}
  \partial_{u^k} F^{k}(w, \lambda^k)
  + \partial_{u^k} F^{k+1}(w, \lambda^{k+1})
  = \partial_{u^k} J(w)
  \qquad \forall w \in V,
  \qquad k = N, N-1, \ldots, 1,
\end{equation}
where, so that the $k = N$ equation takes the same form as the others, we set
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-terminal}
  \lambda^{N+1} := 0.
\end{equation}
\end{defn}

To see how \cref{eq:burgers-adjoint-def} arises, recall the general adjoint equation of \cref{defn:adjoint}:
\begin{equation}
  \partial_{u} F(u, m;\, w, \lambda)
  = \partial_{u} J(u, m;\, w)
  \qquad \forall w \in V.
\end{equation}
Substituting the $k$-th constraint for $F$, so that the state is $u^k$ and the adjoint is $\lambda^k$, gives
\begin{equation}
  \partial_{u^k} F^{k}(w, \lambda^k)
  = \partial_{u^k} J(w)
  \qquad \forall w \in V,
\end{equation}
which is \cref{eq:burgers-adjoint-def} without its second term. In \cref{sec:deriv:general} this was the whole equation, because the state appeared in exactly one constraint. Here the state $u^k$ appears not only in its own step residual $F^k$, but also as the previous state in the next one, $F^{k+1}$, so both residuals contribute a term to the equation for $\lambda^k$; adding the contribution of $F^{k+1}$, tested against its own adjoint $\lambda^{k+1}$, gives the extra term of \cref{eq:burgers-adjoint-def}. This extra term only involves $\lambda^{k+1}$, which is already known when we solve for $\lambda^k$. As a result, the system is solved backward in time, and \cref{eq:burgers-terminal} is the zero terminal condition that starts the backward sweep. Each equation uses the same linearised operator as the corresponding tangent step, with the roles of the direction and the test function swapped, so it is solvable whenever the linearised forward step is.

The cancellation works just as in the general case, with one extra step to tidy up the sums. We test the $n$-th tangent linear equation \cref{eq:burgers-tlm} with $v = \lambda^{n+1}$, and sum over $n = 0, \ldots, N-1$. We then relabel by $k = n + 1$. Since $\delta u^0 = 0$, the term at the bottom of the middle sum drops out, and since $\lambda^{N+1} = 0$, we may append a vanishing term at its top. This gives
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{k=1}^{N}
  \left(
    \partial_{u^k} F^{k}(\delta u^k, \lambda^k)
    + \partial_{u^k} F^{k+1}(\delta u^k, \lambda^{k+1})
  \right)
  = - \sum_{k=1}^{N} \partial_{m^k} F^{k}(\delta m^k, \lambda^k).
\end{equation}
Choosing $w = \delta u^k$ in the $k$-th adjoint equation \cref{eq:burgers-adjoint-def} and summing over $k$, the left-hand side above is exactly $\sum_k \partial_{u^k} J(\delta u^k)$, and so
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-cancellation}
  \sum_{k=1}^{N} \partial_{u^k} J(\delta u^k)
  = - \sum_{k=1}^{N} \partial_{m^k} F^{k}(\delta m^k, \lambda^k).
\end{equation}
Every state variation is gone, at the cost of one backward sweep of adjoint solves, rather than one forward sweep of tangent solves per control direction.

Substituting the partial derivatives \cref{eq:burgers-dFdunew,eq:burgers-dFduold,eq:burgers-Jpartials} into \cref{eq:burgers-adjoint-def} gives the explicit adjoint step: for $k = N, \ldots, 1$, find $\lambda^k \in V$ such that
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-adjoint-weak}
  \int_{\Om}
  \frac{\lambda^k - \lambda^{k+1}}{\Delta t}\, w
  + \left( w \frac{\partial u^k}{\partial x}
           + u^k \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} \right) \lambda^k
  + \nu \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} \frac{\partial \lambda^k}{\partial x}
  \dx
  = \Delta t \int_{\Om} (u^k - d^k)\, w \dx
  \qquad \forall w \in V.
\end{equation}
Each adjoint step is a \emph{linear} spatial problem for $\lambda^k$, even though the forward step was nonlinear. It is linearised about the computed forward state $u^k$, which must therefore be available, so the forward problem is solved and stored first. This is precisely the structure implemented in \cref{sec:burgers-numerics}, namely a nonlinear forward sweep, then a sequence of linear solves backward in time from a zero terminal condition.

Finally, we check consistency with the time-continuous adjoint. Rescaling $\mu^k := \lambda^k / \Delta t$, substituting $\lambda^k = \Delta t\, \mu^k$ into \cref{eq:burgers-adjoint-weak} and dividing through by $\Delta t$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\Om}
  \frac{\mu^k - \mu^{k+1}}{\Delta t}\, w
  + \left( w \frac{\partial u^k}{\partial x}
           + u^k \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} \right) \mu^k
  + \nu \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} \frac{\partial \mu^k}{\partial x}
  \dx
  = \int_{\Om} (u^k - d^k)\, w \dx
  \qquad \forall w \in V.
\end{equation}
Now let $\Delta t \to 0$, with $\mu^k$ approximating $\mu(t_k)$. Since $\mu^{k+1}$ lives at the \emph{later} time $t_{k+1}$, the difference quotient $(\mu^k - \mu^{k+1})/\Delta t$ converges to $-\partial \mu / \partial t$, and the remaining terms converge to their time-continuous counterparts, giving
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\Om}
  -\frac{\partial \mu}{\partial t}\, w
  + \left( w \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}
           + u \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} \right) \mu
  + \nu \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} \frac{\partial \mu}{\partial x}
  \dx
  = \int_{\Om} (u - d)\, w \dx
  \qquad \forall w \in V.
\end{equation}
Integrating the second part of the convection contribution by parts in space, the boundary term vanishes because $w = 0$ on $\dom$, and
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\Om} \left( w \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}
                    + u \frac{\partial w}{\partial x} \right) \mu \dx
  = \int_{\Om} w \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\, \mu \dx
  - \int_{\Om} \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(u \mu)\, w \dx
  = -\int_{\Om} u \frac{\partial \mu}{\partial x}\, w \dx.
\end{equation}
Integrating the diffusion term by parts in the same way moves every spatial derivative off $w$, and the identity becomes $\int_{\Om} \bigl( -\partial_t \mu - u\, \partial_x \mu - \nu\, \partial_{xx} \mu - (u - d) \bigr)\, w \dx = 0$ for every $w \in V$, provided $\mu$ is smooth enough for the second derivative to exist. An integral that vanishes against \emph{every} test function forces the bracket itself to vanish, since if the bracket were nonzero on some small region, a $w$ concentrated on that region would make the integral nonzero \citep[Sect.~5.3]{hc2026}. So $\mu$ satisfies the backward-in-time equation
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-adjoint-strong}
  -\frac{\partial \mu}{\partial t}
  - u \frac{\partial \mu}{\partial x}
  - \nu \frac{\partial^{2} \mu}{\partial x^{2}}
  = u - d
  \qquad \text{in } (0, T) \times \Om,
\end{equation}
with $\mu = 0$ on $(0, T) \times \dom$ and $\mu(T, x) = 0$. This is the adjoint obtained by deriving at the time-continuous level first. The discrete adjoint is therefore consistent with it, but it is the sequence \cref{eq:burgers-adjoint-weak}, not \cref{eq:burgers-adjoint-strong}, that the implementation solves, and it is the sequence that yields the exact derivative of the time-discrete functional we actually minimise.

\subsection{Derivative of the reduced functional and the gradient}
\label{sec:burgers-deriv:reduced}

Substituting the cancellation \cref{eq:burgers-cancellation} into the chain rule turns the derivative into the general formula \cref{eq:dJtilde-general}, applied at each time level and summed. Using $\partial_{m^k} J(\delta m^k) = \alpha \Delta t \inner{m^k}{\delta m^k}{\Lp{\Om}}$ and $\partial_{m^k} F^{k}(\delta m^k, \lambda^k) = -\inner{\delta m^k}{\lambda^k}{\Lp{\Om}}$, this gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-dJtilde}
  \mathrm{d}\Jt(m;\, \delta m)
  = \sum_{k=1}^{N}
    \left(
      \partial_{m^k} J(\delta m^k)
      - \partial_{m^k} F^{k}(\delta m^k, \lambda^k)
    \right)
  = \sum_{k=1}^{N}
    \inner{\lambda^k + \alpha\, \Delta t\, m^k}{\delta m^k}{\Lp{\Om}}
\end{equation}
for all $\delta m \in \Mspace_{\Delta t}$.

To read off the gradient we apply the Riesz map of our chosen control inner product \cref{eq:burgers-control-ip}; the result is the $H$-gradient of \cref{defn:Hgradient} for this choice of $H$. The gradient $g = (g^1, \ldots, g^N)$ is the element of $\Mspace_{\Delta t}$ satisfying $\mathrm{d}\Jt(m;\, \delta m) = \inner{g}{\delta m}{\Delta t} = \Delta t \sum_k \inner{g^k}{\delta m^k}{\Lp{\Om}}$. Comparing with \cref{eq:burgers-dJtilde} level by level gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:burgers-gradient}
  g^k = \alpha\, m^k + \frac{\lambda^k}{\Delta t},
  \qquad k = 1, \ldots, N.
\end{equation}
This is exactly the gradient formula implemented in \cref{sec:burgers-numerics:adjoint}, including the factor $1 / \Delta t$, which the time-continuous derivation does not produce. The reason is that the discrete adjoint $\lambda^k$ carries one factor of $\Delta t$ relative to the continuous adjoint, since it is $\mu^k = \lambda^k / \Delta t$ that converges as $\Delta t \to 0$. The Riesz map of the $\Delta t$-weighted inner product then removes that factor again. In the rescaled variable the gradient reads $g^k = \alpha m^k + \mu^k$, which recovers the same formal structure as the Poisson gradient \cref{eq:l2gradient-poisson}, one time level at a time.

\section{Numerical Implementation and Verification of the Burgers Problem}
\label{sec:burgers-numerics}
% =============================================================================

Having derived the weak formulation, the tangent linear and adjoint equations and the $L^2$-gradient of the reduced functional for the scalar viscous Burgers equation, we now implement the corresponding optimal control problem numerically. The aim is the same as in \cref{sec:poisson-numerics}, namely verifying that the discretisation of the forward problem behaves as expected, and checking that the adjoint-based gradient computation is correct.

In contrast to what we did with the Poisson problem, where we provided both a plain Python implementation and a Firedrake implementation, we treat the Burgers problem at the lower level only. The main new difficulties specific to Burgers' equation are the nonlinearity, the time dependence and the backward-in-time adjoint solve. Implementing these ingredients directly in plain Python makes the structure of the method more transparent before moving to a higher-level framework.

\subsection{Scalar Burgers numerically}
\label{sec:burgers-numerics:byhand}

We consider the one-dimensional spatial domain $\Om = (0, 1)$ and a final time $T > 0$. The implementation discretises the scalar viscous Burgers equation in space using continuous piecewise linear finite elements on a uniform mesh, and in time using the backward Euler method of \cref{sec:burgers-deriv:setup}, on a uniform grid of $N_t$ steps of size $\Delta t = T / N_t$.

At each implicit time level, the nonlinear problem is solved using Newton's method. If $U^n$ denotes the finite element coefficient vector at time $t_n$, then the fully discrete forward problem at $t_{n+1}$ has the form
\begin{equation}
  M_h \frac{U^{n+1} - U^{n}}{\Delta t}
  + C(U^{n+1}) + \nu K_h U^{n+1} = b^{n+1},
\end{equation}
where $M_h$ is the finite element mass matrix, $K_h$ is the stiffness matrix, $C(U^{n+1})$ is the nonlinear convection contribution, and $b^{n+1}$ is the load vector associated with the source term or control at time $t_{n+1}$.

As with the plain Python Poisson implementation, this code prioritises clarity over efficiency. Writing the assembly, the time stepping and the Newton linearisation explicitly makes the path from the nonlinear weak form to the algebraic system solved at each time step explicit. The implementation imposes homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions strongly at the two endpoints of the interval.

\subsection{Manufactured solution and convergence study}
\label{sec:burgers-numerics:mms}

To verify the forward implementation, we again use the method of manufactured solutions. We prescribe a smooth exact solution satisfying the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, and choose the corresponding source term so that this exact solution satisfies the Burgers equation.

We define
\begin{equation}
  u_{\mathrm{exact}}(t, x) = e^{-t} \sin(\pi x),
\end{equation}
which vanishes at $x = 0$ and $x = 1$ and therefore satisfies the boundary conditions. Substituting into the strong form \cref{eq:burgers-strong} gives the manufactured source term
\begin{equation}
  m_{\mathrm{exact}}(t, x)
  = - e^{-t} \sin(\pi x)
  + \pi e^{-2t} \sin(\pi x) \cos(\pi x)
  + \nu \pi^{2} e^{-t} \sin(\pi x).
\end{equation}

We solve the numerical problem on a sequence of uniformly refined meshes with $N \in \{10, 20, 40, 80\}$ elements. Since the problem is time-dependent, the temporal discretisation is refined at the same rate by taking $N_t = 8N$, so that $\Delta t$ remains proportional to the mesh size $h$.

We record two error measures: the final-time $\Lp{\Om}$ error,
\begin{equation}
  e_h^{\mathrm{final}} := \|u_h(T) - u_{\mathrm{exact}}(T)\|_{\Lp{\Om}},
\end{equation}
and a fully discrete space-time error,
\begin{equation}
  e_h^{\mathrm{st}}
  := \left( \Delta t \sum_{n=1}^{N_t}
              \|u_h^n - u_{\mathrm{exact}}(t_n)\|_{\Lp{\Om}}^{2}
        \right)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
The experimental order of convergence between two successive refinements is
\begin{equation}
  q = \frac{\log(e_H / e_h)}{\log(h_H / h_h)}.
\end{equation}

The observed results are shown in \cref{tab:burgers-mms}.

\subsection{Discrete adjoint solve and gradient computation}
\label{sec:burgers-numerics:adjoint}

We implement the discrete adjoint equation and compute the gradient of the reduced functional at the fully discrete level, consistent with the backward Euler time discretisation of the forward problem.

If $D^n$ denotes the discrete desired state at time $t_n$ and $m^n$ the discrete control, the fully discrete tracking functional, the mass-matrix representation of the objective \cref{eq:burgers-objective}, is
\begin{equation}
  J_h(U, m)
  = \frac{\Delta t}{2} \sum_{n=1}^{N_t} \|U^n - D^n\|_{M_h}^{2}
  + \frac{\alpha \Delta t}{2} \sum_{n=1}^{N_t} \|m^n\|_{M_h}^{2},
\end{equation}
where $\|v\|_{M_h}^{2} := v^{T} M_h v$ is the mass-matrix representation of the $\Lp{\Om}$ norm.

We generate the desired state by first choosing a smooth reference control, solving the forward Burgers problem for that control, and taking the resulting discrete state trajectory as the target trajectory. We then choose a different trial control and solve the forward problem again in order to compute the state, the objective value, the adjoint and the gradient.

The adjoint solve follows the structure derived in \cref{sec:burgers-deriv:adjoint}. The nonlinear forward problem is solved first and its states stored, since the adjoint is linearised about them, and the adjoint systems \cref{eq:burgers-adjoint-weak}, restricted to the finite element space, are then solved backward in time from the zero terminal condition \citep[Sect.~1.4.5]{shfp2017}.

The resulting discrete gradient has the form
\begin{equation}
  g^n = \alpha m^n + \frac{1}{\Delta t} p^n,
\end{equation}
where $p^n$ is the discrete adjoint variable at time level $t_n$. This is exactly the gradient formula \cref{eq:burgers-gradient} derived in \cref{sec:burgers-deriv:reduced}, with $p^n$ the spatial discretisation of $\lambda^n$.

For the representative trial control used in the experiments, the computed objective value was
\begin{equation}
  J_h(m) = 1.323574 \times 10^{-4}.
\end{equation}
This is the baseline value that the Taylor test of \cref{sec:burgers-numerics:taylor} differences against, and it sets the scale for reading the remainders of \cref{tab:burgers-taylor}. The average number of Newton iterations required in the forward solve was approximately $3$ per time step, indicating that the nonlinear solver converged reliably for the chosen problem parameters.

\subsection{Taylor test for gradient verification}
\label{sec:burgers-numerics:taylor}

After implementing the discrete adjoint and gradient, we carry out a Taylor test in the same spirit as in \cref{sec:poisson-numerics:taylor}.

Let $\delta m$ be a perturbation direction in the discrete control space and let $h > 0$. The reduced functional satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \Jt_h(m + h \delta m)
  = \Jt_h(m) + h\, \mathrm{d}\Jt_h(m;\, \delta m)
  + \mathcal{O}(h^{2}),
\end{equation}
and if the gradient implementation is correct, the second-order remainder
\begin{equation}
  R_2(h)
  := \left|
       \Jt_h(m + h \delta m) - \Jt_h(m)
       - h\, \mathrm{d}\Jt_h(m;\, \delta m)
     \right|
\end{equation}
should decay as $\mathcal{O}(h^{2})$ as $h \to 0$.

Using a smooth perturbation direction normalised in the discrete control inner product, we obtained the results of \cref{tab:burgers-taylor}.

% =============================================================================
\section{Mesh Dependence in PDE-Constrained Optimisation}
\label{sec:mesh-dependence}
% =============================================================================

So far, we have derived, discretised and verified the adjoint-based gradient for both the Poisson and the scalar viscous Burgers optimal control problems. In each case, the $L^2$-gradient $\grad \Jt(m) = \lambda + \alpha m$ was identified using the Riesz representation theorem (\cref{thm:riesz}). However, that identification is not unique, since it depends on the choice of Hilbert space structure used for the control space $\Mspace$. In this section we will look at the practical consequences of that choice when the reduced functional is minimised numerically. Following \citet[Chap.~2]{shfp2017}, we show that two different but mathematically valid choices of inner product give optimisation algorithms whose iteration counts behave very differently as the mesh is refined.

\subsection{The two Riesz maps}
\label{sec:meshdep:background}

In \Cref{sec:prelim:riesz,sec:deriv:l2grad}, we have already established that the Fr{\'e}chet derivative $\mathrm{d}\Jt(m)$ is an element of the dual space $\Mspace^{*}$, and that the Riesz map $\Rmap_H : \Mspace^{*} \to \Mspace$ identifies each derivative with a primal element of $\Mspace$. Which primal element it returns depends on the inner product we give $\Mspace$, and there are two natural candidates: when $\Mspace = \Lp{\Om}$ is treated as an $L^{2}$ Hilbert space, the Riesz map $\Rmap_{L^2}$ corresponds in the discrete setting to multiplication by the inverse of the Galerkin mass matrix \citep[(1.125)]{shfp2017}. When the same coefficient vector is taken instead as an element of $\R^{d}$ under the standard Euclidean ($\ell^{2}$) inner product, no mass matrix appears and the Riesz map is the identity.

The two choices give us primal gradients that differ at every mesh refinement: the $\ell^{2}$ representer carries the local cell sizes encoded in the diagonal of the mass matrix, while the $L^{2}$ representer does not. So, we can see that search directions and stopping criteria based on gradient norms behave differently in the two settings. It matters, then, which of the two an optimisation package uses internally, and whether the user can change it. \Citet[Sect.~2.1]{shfp2017} note that ``many well-established continuous optimisation packages are not designed for function-based optimisation and are hard-coded to apply the Euclidean ($\ell^{2}$) inner product''. That assessment dates from 2017 and is now only partly accurate, as we show later in this section.

\subsection{Non-uniform meshes and the mesh ratio}
\label{sec:meshdep:why-meshes}
\label{sec:meshdep:hypothesis}

Before stating the prediction we test, it is worth being precise about the meshes on which mesh dependence occurs at all, since what determines it turns out not to be how fine a mesh is, but how uneven it is.

The accuracy of a finite element solution is controlled by the size of each cell together with how rapidly the solution varies inside it \citep[Sect.~3.4]{hc2026}. A uniform mesh must therefore be fine enough everywhere to resolve the most demanding part of the solution. However, that is expensive, since in two dimensions halving the cell size multiplies the number of cells, and with them the cost of every forward and adjoint solve, by four. Practical meshes are non-uniform instead, with small cells where the solution has fine structure and large cells where it is smooth. A solution may change sharply in a thin strip next to the boundary, or become arbitrarily steep at a corner that points into the domain, such as the inner corner of an L-shaped region. It may also carry a steep front that moves, as the Burgers solution of \cref{sec:burgers-weak} does. Non-uniform meshes are therefore not an edge case constructed to stress test an optimiser, but instead what one would generally compute on in practice.

A concrete example makes the trade-off explicit. Consider the steady temperature in a cross-section of a room, heated by a small radiator against one wall and losing heat through a window set into the wall opposite. We model each as a narrow Gaussian, small compared with the room. The temperature is then nearly uniform across most of the room and varies sharply in two small regions, as the left panel of \cref{fig:heat-source-meshes} shows. This is the situation where a graded mesh is required, as the solution needs many cells at the radiator and the window, and almost none in between.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/heat_source_meshes.pdf}
  \caption{A room heated by a radiator and cooled through a window. The temperature field is nearly uniform across the room and varies sharply only at the two features (left). A uniform mesh spends its cells evenly and resolves neither feature well (centre). Grading the same background resolution towards both (right) reduces the $L^{2}$ error by a factor of about $31$ for roughly seven times the cells, at the cost of a mesh ratio of $32$.}
  \label{fig:heat-source-meshes}
\end{figure}

To measure the uniformity of a mesh, recall the cell diameter $h_K$ of \cref{defn:mesh} and set
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:meshdep:hmax-hmin}
  h_{\max} := \max_{K \in \mathcal{T}_h} h_K
  \qquad \text{and} \qquad
  h_{\min} := \min_{K \in \mathcal{T}_h} h_K
\end{equation}
to be the diameters of the largest and smallest cells in the mesh. The \emph{mesh ratio} $h_{\max} / h_{\min}$ equals one for a perfectly uniform mesh and grows as the grading becomes more severe, and we will take it as a measure of the non-uniformity of the mesh. What matters for the argument that follows is that the ratio records only how unequal the cells are, and not how many of them there are. That distinction is the reason the mesh ratio, rather than the mesh size, indexes every experiment in this section. 

We test the prediction of \citet[Sect.~2.2]{shfp2017}, namely that, for a fixed gradient-norm convergence threshold, the iteration count of a quasi-Newton method depends on which Riesz map is used to interpret the derivative as a primal-space gradient. Concretely:

\begin{description}
  \item[(I)] When the $\ell^{2}$ Riesz map is used, the iteration count grows with the mesh ratio $h_{\max} / h_{\min}$ (mesh-dependent convergence).
  \item[(II)] When the $L^{2}$ Riesz map is used, the iteration count is bounded uniformly in $h_{\max} / h_{\min}$ (mesh-independent convergence).
\end{description}

\subsection{Mesh generation}
\label{sec:meshdep:meshes}

We use two families of non-uniform mesh. First, we use deterministic graded meshes because they are the meshes behind Table~2.2 of \citet{shfp2017}, whose refinement is fixed and whose $h_{\max}/h_{\min}$ ratio can be set to a prescribed value, which is what a faithful reproduction of the headline result requires. The randomly refined meshes come second, as a robustness check. They discard the smooth, structured grading of the first family and refine cells irregularly, so that any mesh-dependence we observe cannot be a side-effect of one tidy grading pattern; if the same behaviour survives on irregular meshes, it is a property of the inner product rather than of the mesh construction. We describe the two families in turn.

The deterministic graded meshes are built on a structured base. The unit square is meshed by Firedrake's \texttt{UnitSquareMesh} function into an $8 \times 8$ grid of squares, each split into two triangles along a diagonal. A fixed interior sub-square $[0.4, 0.6]^{2}$ is then refined by repeated longest-edge (Rivara) bisection \citep{rivara1984}: in each round, every triangle whose centroid lies in the sub-square is bisected, and the bisection is propagated to neighbours so that the mesh stays conforming, with no hanging nodes. After $2\log_{2} r$ rounds the cells inside the sub-square are a factor $r$ finer than those outside, so the realised ratio $h_{\max}/h_{\min}$ is \emph{exactly} the prescribed power of two rather than an approximation to it. Three of the six meshes are shown in \cref{fig:graded-meshes}.

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{sec:meshdep:implementation}

Testing the hypothesis in code requires pinning down two things, namely which inner product each algorithm uses internally, and a stopping criterion applied identically to every algorithm regardless of that choice. We describe how each is handled.

The experiments are built on Firedrake~\citep{firedrake2016} and its algorithmic-differentiation extension pyadjoint~\citep{mitusch2019}. The choice of inner product on the control is made when the control is declared, by giving pyadjoint's \texttt{Control} a \texttt{riesz\_map} of \texttt{"l2"}, \texttt{"L2"} or \texttt{"H1"}, which selects the discrete Riesz map applied when the dual derivative $\mathrm{d}\Jt(m)$ is converted into a primal gradient:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
Jhat = ReducedFunctional(
    J, Control(m, riesz_map=riesz_map), tape=tape,
)
\end{lstlisting}
That single argument is the experimental variable of this section. The problem, the mesh, the forward solver and the stopping threshold are all held fixed, so any difference in iteration count between two runs must be due to it alone. The forward Poisson problem is solved by a direct LU factorisation, so the discrete gradient is computed to machine precision, and the convergence threshold $\varepsilon = 10^{-7}$ on $\|\Rmap_H(J')\|_H$ is therefore never obscured by Krylov-solver noise.

Following \citet[pp.~67--68]{shfp2017}, we apply the convergence criterion panel-wide rather than per algorithm, meaning that every algorithm in a panel is stopped on the same gradient norm $\|\Rmap_H(J')\|_H \le 10^{-7}$, regardless of the inner product it uses internally. This is what makes the rows comparable, since an algorithm allowed to stop on its own norm would be answering a different question from the one beside it. For an algorithm hard-coded to the $\ell^{2}$ inner product, such as SciPy's L-BFGS-B, the norm is therefore evaluated \emph{outside} the algorithm, by disabling its own tolerances so that they never trigger and halting the run from an external check on $\|\Rmap_H(J')\|_H$ at each iteration.

The problem can also be routed through PETSc's TAO solver, which pyadjoint supports directly. We tried this first, since it is much the easiest route, but it did not reproduce the function-space row, and working out why turned into a study of its own. That is \cref{sec:meshdep:tao-revisited}. The function-space rows of the experiments are produced instead by our own limited-memory BFGS, which follows \citet[Sect.~1.5.4.1]{shfp2017} and is stated in \cref{sec:meshdep:hilbert-lbfgs}.

\subsection{BFGS in a Hilbert space}
\label{sec:meshdep:hilbert-lbfgs}

The hypothesis of \cref{sec:meshdep:why-meshes} is a claim about a single ingredient of a quasi-Newton method, namely the inner product it uses to turn a derivative into a search direction. Testing it convincingly needs an implementation where that ingredient is visible rather than buried, so that changing it is clearly the only thing that has changed. Neither package in \cref{tab:schwedes22} serves that purpose, since SciPy is hard-coded to $\ell^{2}$ and TAO's update runs inside a PETSc kernel that cannot readily be inspected from Python. So, we write the algorithm out ourselves, as a limited-memory BFGS where the metric is completely explicit, so that one can read off at every step where the inner product enters. This is the implementation that produces the function-space row of every experiment that follows.

We take $H$ to be either $\Lp{\Om}$ or $H^{1}(\Om)$, with the inner products
\begin{equation}
  \inner{a}{b}{L^{2}} = \int_{\Om} a\, b \dx,
  \qquad
  \inner{a}{b}{H^{1}}
     = \int_{\Om} \bigl(a\, b + \grad a \cdot \grad b\bigr) \dx.
\end{equation}
Limited-memory BFGS never forms an approximate inverse Hessian explicitly. It keeps a short history of curvature pairs and applies the approximation to a vector through a two-loop recursion, which is no more than a sequence of inner products and vector updates. That structure is what makes the metric easy to locate, since the inner product appears in every one of those products and nowhere else, so replacing $\ell^{2}$ by $\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}{H}$ throughout is the entire modification.

At the current \emph{iterate} $m_{k} \in \Mspace$, meaning the optimiser's current approximation to the optimal control after $k$ steps, let $g_{k} := \Rmap_H(\mathrm{d}\Jt(m_{k}))$ denote the primal Riesz representer of the derivative. The algorithm stores the most recent $\ell \le 5$ curvature pairs
\begin{equation}
  s_{j} := m_{j+1} - m_{j} \in \Mspace,
  \qquad
  y_{j} := g_{j+1} - g_{j} \in \Mspace,
  \qquad
  \rho_{j} := \frac{1}{\inner{s_{j}}{y_{j}}{H}},
\end{equation}
and forms the search direction $p_k$ by the two-loop recursion
\begin{align}
  q & \leftarrow g_{k}, \\
  \intertext{for $j = k-1, k-2, \ldots, k-\ell$:}
  \alpha_{j} & \leftarrow \rho_{j}\, \inner{s_{j}}{q}{H}, \\
  q          & \leftarrow q - \alpha_{j}\, y_{j}, \\
  \intertext{then}
  z          & \leftarrow \gamma_{k}\, q, \\
  \intertext{for $j = k-\ell, \ldots, k-1$:}
  \beta_{j}  & \leftarrow \rho_{j}\, \inner{y_{j}}{z}{H}, \\
  z          & \leftarrow z + (\alpha_{j} - \beta_{j})\, s_{j}, \\
  \intertext{and finally}
  p_{k}      & \leftarrow -z,
\end{align}
in which every inner product is taken in $\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}{H}$.

The recursion begins not from the identity but from an initial inverse Hessian $H_{0} = \gamma_{k} I$, rescaled at each iteration by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:meshdep:gamma_k}
  \gamma_{k}
  = \frac{\inner{s_{k-1}}{y_{k-1}}{H}}{\inner{y_{k-1}}{y_{k-1}}{H}},
\end{equation}
the standard L-BFGS scaling of \citet[Sect.~1.5.4.2]{shfp2017}, which adapts it to the local curvature. Omitting it, that is taking $\gamma_{k} = 1$, causes the algorithm to plateau several orders of magnitude above the threshold, even on a well-conditioned mesh.

The step length is chosen by a backtracking Armijo line search where, starting from $\alpha = 1$, the trial step is halved until the sufficient-decrease condition
\begin{equation}
  \Jt(m_{k} + \alpha\, p_{k})
    \le \Jt(m_{k})
       + c_{1}\, \alpha\, \inner{g_{k}}{p_{k}}{H},
  \qquad c_{1} = 10^{-4},
\end{equation}
holds. Curvature pairs with $\inner{s_{j}}{y_{j}}{H} \le 0$ are discarded, and the iteration terminates once $\|g_{k}\|_{H} \le \varepsilon = 10^{-7}$.

The Riesz representer $g_k$ is obtained from the dual derivative by a direct LU factorisation of the inner-product matrix, $M_h$ for $L^{2}$ and $M_h + K_h$ for $H^{1}$. An iterative solve would be cheaper, but it would stop at a tolerance of its own, and whatever error it left behind would sit inside the very gradient norm that the convergence test measures. Since that test is applied at $\varepsilon = 10^{-7}$, a stalled optimiser would then be indistinguishable from an inaccurate Riesz map. The direct factorisation is accurate to machine precision, so no such ambiguity arises.

\subsection{Numerical experiments: Panel (a), \texorpdfstring{$H = \Lp{\Om}$}{H = L2}}
\label{sec:meshdep:panel-a}

With both implementations in place, we can run the comparison the hypothesis asks for, changing the inner product and holding everything else fixed. We reproduce Panel~(a) of Table~2.2 in \citet{shfp2017}, in which the control space is treated as the $L^{2}$ Hilbert space.

The state equation that we use is the weak Poisson problem \cref{eq:weak-forward-compact}, and the objective is \cref{eq:objective-J} with target $d(x, y) = \sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)$ and Tikhonov parameter $\alpha = 10^{-4}$. \subsection{Numerical experiments: Panel (b), \texorpdfstring{$H = H^{1}(\Om)$}{H = H1}}
\label{sec:meshdep:panel-b}

Panel~(a) fixed the inner product to $L^{2}$. The hypothesis is a claim about the choice of inner product rather than about $L^{2}$ in particular, so it should still hold even if we replace that choice with another one. Panel~(b) of Table~2.2 in \citet{shfp2017} repeats the experiment with the $H^{1}$ inner product on the control space. Before setting the experiment up there is one thing we need to get right, and it makes a large difference to the result. The objective functional of \citet[(1.219)]{shfp2017} regularises in the \emph{same} norm as the metric. It reads $\tfrac{1}{2}\|u - d\|_{L^{2}}^{2} + \tfrac{\alpha}{2}\|m\|_{\Mspace}^{2}$ with $\Mspace = L^{2}$ or $H^{1}$, so the norm appears twice, and changing $\Mspace$ changes both. Moving from Panel~(a) to Panel~(b) therefore changes the regularisation term from $\tfrac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Om} m^{2}$ to
\begin{equation}
  \tfrac{\alpha}{2}\|m\|_{H^{1}}^{2}
    = \tfrac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\Om}\bigl(m^{2} + |\grad m|^{2}\bigr)\dx.
\end{equation}
We pose this $H^{1}$-regularised problem and, as in Panel~(a), compare two algorithms stopped on the same threshold $\varepsilon = 10^{-7}$, now measured in the $H^{1}$ norm $\|\Rmap_{H^{1}}(J')\|_{H^{1}}$, namely SciPy's $\ell^{2}$ L-BFGS-B, with the convergence test performed externally in $H^{1}$, and the Hilbert-space L-BFGS of \cref{sec:meshdep:hilbert-lbfgs} with $H = H^{1}(\Om)$. The results are given in \cref{tab:meshdep:panel-b}.

Applying the $H^{1}$ metric to the $L^{2}$-regularised problem of Panel~(a), that is, changing the inner product without changing the objective, the Hilbert-space L-BFGS still reaches the same $L^{2}$-regularised optimum, but takes $100, 102, 103, 136, 113$ and $141$ iterations across the six meshes. \subsection{The limited-memory history in TAO/LMVM}
\label{sec:meshdep:tao-revisited}

The two algorithms of Panels~(a) and~(b) were chosen to make the comparison clean, not to be representative. As we know, SciPy cannot be given a different inner product at all, and the Hilbert-space L-BFGS was written by us precisely so that it would use the right one. So, neither of these tells us what a user of this software stack would actually use.

Something that the user could meet is TAO. This is the optimisation component of PETSc, which already underpins every linear and nonlinear solve in Firedrake, so it comes with any Firedrake installation rather than being an extra dependency, and pyadjoint ships a wrapper that drives it directly. It also offers a whole library of methods rather than a single one. It is also one of the three packages \citet[Sect.~2.1]{shfp2017} name as hard-coded to the Euclidean inner product. It is therefore the natural place to ask whether that is still true.

The method we use throughout this subsection is TAO's \texttt{lmvm}, short for limited-memory variable metric, which is its own implementation of the limited-memory BFGS of \cref{sec:meshdep:hilbert-lbfgs}. There is an obvious way to check whether it can be given the right inner product. Declaring the control as \texttt{Control(m, riesz\_map="L2")} makes pyadjoint install the discrete $L^{2}$ Riesz map inside the solver, so if the inner product were the only obstacle, that single argument should be enough to reproduce the flat Moola row. It is not. \Cref{tab:meshdep:lmvm-hist5} gives the counts on the deterministic graded meshes of \cref{tab:meshdep:mesh_summary}, with every other setting left at its default.

Note that this is not what we expected. The counts are large, but they do not grow with the mesh. They rise once, from $83$ to $173$, and then settle at $156$ even as the mesh ratio goes up by a further factor of eight and the cell count by a factor of fifty. The seed is doing its job, in that the run is already somewhat mesh-independent, but what is wrong is the size of the constant rather than its growth. So, we can see that the metric does indeed reach the algorithm, but there is still some fault, which we investigate.

It could be that the stall was a noise floor, a level below which round-off in the adjoint tape swamps the true gradient, so that no amount of further iteration helps. A noise floor belongs to the gradient computation rather than to the optimiser, so every solver reading the same tape would meet the same wall, and that is what makes it testable by changing the solver. We replaced TAO/LMVM with TAO/NLS, an exact Newton method that builds each step from conjugate-gradient (CG) Hessian-vector products, and tightened the tolerance to $\varepsilon = 10^{-30}$. The Poisson problem is quadratic, so a single Newton step lands on the discrete optimum. At an optimum the true gradient is zero, so whatever the tape reports there is nothing but numerical error, which is exactly the quantity we want to measure. It reported $\|\Rmap_{L^{2}}(J')\|_{L^{2}} \approx 10^{-18}$ on every mesh in the sweep. The floor is therefore at machine precision, far below where LMVM had stalled, and since both solvers read the same tape it cannot be what stopped LMVM. The same run says something more useful. An exact-Hessian method reaches the optimum in a single step, so the problem itself is not hard; what is expensive must be the way LMVM approximates that Hessian.

A second idea is that the limited memory is at fault, in that a rank-five quasi-Newton update is too coarse for the Hessian spectrum on a graded mesh. Our first test of this was itself invalid, because it used a synthetic exponential stretch, which collapses cells onto the Dirichlet corner where both the state $u$ and the target $d$ vanish. The over-resolved region there carries no information about the objective, so part of the apparent stalling came from the setup rather than from the solver.

The Newton probe points squarely at the limited memory. A rank-five update simply cannot capture enough of the Hessian spectrum on a graded mesh, so each step is poor and many of them are needed, but the metric is still being respected throughout. The fix is therefore to keep the seed and lengthen the history.

The history size is awkward to set in practice. Passing \texttt{tao\_lmvm\_mat\_lmvm\_hist\_size} through pyadjoint's \texttt{parameters} dictionary does not propagate to the LMVM sub-matrix, because of an options-prefix mismatch between pyadjoint and the internal PETSc matrix object attached to the LMVM \texttt{Tao}. The option must instead be registered on the global PETSc options database before the \texttt{TAOSolver} is constructed,
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
PETSc.Options().setValue('-tao_lmvm_mat_lmvm_hist_size', 100)
\end{lstlisting}
or, alternatively, the pyadjoint TAO solver may be constructed with an empty options prefix. We use the former throughout.

Setting the history to $100$ (and leaving everything else the same) gives \cref{tab:meshdep:lmvm-hist100}.

The seed carries the metric and is what makes the count flat; the history decides how large that flat count is. There is a limit to what these two tables can show, however. Because \texttt{lmvm} is seeded in every run above, they vary the history alone, and so cannot tell us what the seed contributes by itself. Two things justify stopping where we did. The first is cost. Each iteration needs a forward solve and an adjoint solve, so the $r = 32$ run took forty minutes, and the $r = 64$ run was still short of the tolerance after more than nine hours. Because the growth factor is itself increasing, from $1.4$ to $2.2$ to $2.7$ across the sweep, a run at $r = 128$ on four times as many cells would take days rather than hours.

The second is that further runs would not change the conclusion. The four converged points already span nearly an order of magnitude, on meshes where every other solver in \cref{tab:meshdep:tao-zoo} finishes in under $130$ iterations. The pattern across the table is clear, with one caveat. The Newton-class solvers are flat, and so is \texttt{lmvm} once its history is fixed, while nonlinear conjugate gradient grows steeply. Each of those steps hides an inner Krylov solve that this table does not count, and \cref{sec:precond} shows that the inner solve grows from $61$ to $1052$ iterations across this same sweep. For the Newton-class solvers the mesh-dependence has moved out of view rather than gone away. Whatever causes the growth is therefore not peculiar to LMVM, and it is not shared by every solver either. One row is left over: \texttt{bqnls} is mesh-dependent even though it is a quasi-Newton method like \texttt{lmvm}, and we will look into this further in \cref{sec:meshdep:bqnls-isolation}.

One caveat applies to all of this. The Poisson problem is quadratic in $m$, and quasi-Newton methods do unusually well on quadratics whatever inner product they use, so the survey ought to be repeated on a problem that is not quadratic. The obvious candidate is the one-dimensional Burgers control problem, and we attempted it, but that run used the synthetic exponentially-stretched interval whose cells collapse onto the Dirichlet endpoint, the same problem as above, so its results are not included. A direct replacement is not available, because the graded-mesh construction used here is two-dimensional. Later work could cluster cells towards an interior point of the interval instead, or move to a two-dimensional Burgers problem on the same meshes as the Poisson problem.

On the Poisson evidence, then, what decides the outcome is the metric rather than the family a solver belongs to. Every solver here that can be given the function-space inner product is flat, and every one that cannot is not. The Newton-class rows only appear to escape this, and the price they pay is deferred to the inner solve that \cref{sec:precond} takes up.

\subsection{Isolation of the seed in bqnls}
\label{sec:meshdep:bqnls-isolation}

The diagnosis above leaves a question that deserves its own treatment, namely why installing the mass-matrix seed makes \texttt{bqnls} mesh-independent. This section and the next answer it, using the conditioning machinery that \citet[Sect.~2.2]{shfp2017} developed for steepest descent. We first pin down experimentally which ingredient does the work.

Two pieces of notation are needed for what follows. TAO calls the initial Hessian approximation of a limited-memory method its $J_{0}$, and this is what we have been calling the seed. Setting $J_{0} = M_h$, with $M_h$ the finite element mass matrix of \cref{sec:burgers-numerics:byhand}, therefore starts the recursion from the mass matrix rather than from the identity, and it is the mass matrix because $M_h^{-1}$ is the discrete $L^{2}$ Riesz map.

The two candidate ingredients are the seed and the history length, so we ran \texttt{bqnls} in all four combinations on the deterministic graded meshes of \cref{tab:meshdep:mesh_summary}, at the usual threshold $\varepsilon = 10^{-7}$ on $\|\Rmap_{L^{2}}(J')\|_{L^{2}}$. The results are collected in \cref{tab:meshdep:bqnls-isolation}.

The table separates the two ingredients completely. Without the seed the count grows with the mesh ratio whether the history is five or one hundred, so no amount of memory rescues the run. With the seed the count is a flat six whether the history is five or one hundred, so almost no memory is needed. The seed does all of the work, and whatever the seed provides, six directions' worth of memory is enough to exploit it. 

What the seed provides is an inner product. The two-loop recursion pairs a primal step with a dual gradient difference, which is a duality pairing and carries no metric. The single metric-carrying operation is the seed, applied between the two sweeps, as we will confirm by reading the source in \cref{sec:lbfgs:tao}. Installing $J_0 = M_h$ makes the recursion apply $J_0^{-1} = M_h^{-1} = \Rmap_{L^{2}}$, the discrete Riesz map, at exactly that point. By \citet[(1.208)--(1.210)]{shfp2017} this turns the Euclidean L-BFGS update into the Hilbert-space update in $L^{2}$. Seeded \texttt{bqnls} is therefore not a better-tuned $\ell^{2}$ method but one applying a different operator, the same update as the hand-written reference of \cref{sec:meshdep:hilbert-lbfgs}, though the two still differ in their line search and in the bound handling. Consistently with that, it took the same six iterations as that reference did in \cref{tab:meshdep:panel-a}, which was itself run with a history of five.

One distinction is worth making clear. This is the same operator $M_h^{-1}$ that \cref{sec:meshdep:inner-cg} installs as a preconditioner on the inner Krylov solve of the Newton solver, but it enters at a different point. Since \texttt{bqnls} performs no Hessian solve, there is no Krylov iteration to precondition, and the metric can only be supplied as the quasi-Newton seed. The same Riesz map is supplied at two different points of two different algorithms; the principle connecting them is that choosing an inner product and preconditioning are the same act \citep[pp.~55--56]{shfp2017}, which is the classical operator-preconditioning principle \citep{kirby2010, mardal2011}.

\subsection{The reduced Hessian in the two inner products}
\label{sec:meshdep:bqnls-why}

The previous section showed by experiment that the seed is what makes the count flat, and that no amount of memory substitutes for it. This section explains why, by looking at the spectrum of the Hessian that each run actually sees. The argument is the one \citet[Sect.~2.2]{shfp2017} give for steepest descent, applied here to a quasi-Newton method, and it also accounts for the more surprising half of the experiment, namely why a longer history cannot make up for a missing seed.

The Poisson problem is quadratic in the control, and the behaviour of a BFGS-type method on a quadratic is governed by the spectrum of the Hessian measured in the inner product the method works in. The two halves of \cref{tab:meshdep:bqnls-isolation} therefore see two different spectra of the same problem. Write $K_h$ and $M_h$ for the stiffness and mass matrices, and $\mathbf{m}$ for the coefficient vector of the control. The discrete state is then $U = K_h^{-1} M_h \mathbf{m}$, and the Hessian of the discrete reduced functional, as a function of $\mathbf{m}$, is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:meshdep:bqnls-hessian}
  H \;=\; M_h K_h^{-1} M_h K_h^{-1} M_h + \alpha M_h
    \;=\; M_h \left( S_h^{2} + \alpha I \right),
  \qquad S_h := K_h^{-1} M_h .
\end{equation}
This factorisation is the key point, since every cell-size scaling in $H$ sits in the leading mass-matrix factor, exactly (not just approximately) so for the regularisation term $\alpha M_h$, while the remaining factor $S_h^{2} + \alpha I$ is the matrix of the reduced Hessian operator in the $L^{2}$ inner product.

The unseeded run works in $\ell^{2}$, so it sees the spectrum of $H$ itself, which inherits the conditioning of $M_h$. This is precisely the mechanism that \citet[Sect.~2.2.4]{shfp2017} analyse for steepest descent. For their model problem the Hessian \emph{equals} the mass matrix, and \citet[Remark~2.2.4.1]{shfp2017} extends the analysis to any objective containing an $\alpha \inner{u}{u}{H}$ term, as ours does. The local mass matrix of an element scales with its volume, $M_K = h_1 \cdots h_n \hat{M}$ \citep[(2.36)]{shfp2017}, where $\hat{M}$ is the reference-element mass matrix. This gives the condition number bound
\begin{equation}
  \kappa(M_h) \;\le\; p_{\max}\,
  \frac{\lambda_{\max}^{\hat M}}{\lambda_{\min}^{\hat M}}
  \left( \frac{h_{\max}}{h_{\min}} \right)^{\! n},
\end{equation}
with $p_{\max}$ the maximum number of elements meeting at a node \citep[(2.40)]{shfp2017}, and from it an iteration count for steepest descent that is polynomial in $h_{\max}/h_{\min}$ of order the domain dimension $n$ \citep[(2.44)]{shfp2017}. On our two-dimensional meshes the bound grows like $(h_{\max}/h_{\min})^{2}$, reaching roughly $1.6 \times 10^{4}$ at $r = 128$. \citet{shfp2017} derive the estimate for steepest descent rather than for L-BFGS, so for \texttt{bqnls} we take from it the mechanism rather than the exponent; the observed unseeded row ($30, 76, 130, 163$) grows with the ratio exactly as the mechanism predicts.

The same picture shows why a longer history cannot substitute for the seed. A limited-memory method with memory $\ell$ corrects its seed by an update of rank at most $2\ell$, so it can repair a spectrum that is wrong in a few directions. The spread of $M_h$ is not of that kind, since the cell volumes vary continuously across the graded mesh, one scale per region and thousands of cells in all, so no moderate-rank correction of the identity can approximate $M_h^{-1}$. The history-$100$ row of \cref{tab:meshdep:bqnls-isolation} does keep growing.

The seeded run works in $L^{2}$, so it sees the generalised spectrum of $H$ relative to $M_h$, which by \cref{eq:meshdep:bqnls-hessian} is the spectrum of $S_h^{2} + \alpha I$. The matrix $S_h$ discretises the solution operator $S = (-\Delta)^{-1}$, which is compact. The spectrum is therefore a cluster at $\alpha$ together with a small number of decaying outliers, and it converges to the spectrum of the continuous operator as the mesh refines, however the mesh is graded. This is the same clustered spectrum that makes the Riesz-preconditioned inner CG mesh-independent (\cref{sec:precond:spectral}); here it acts one level up, on the outer quasi-Newton iteration. The seeded run therefore faces a fixed handful of significant directions, whatever the grading, which is why its count is small, flat, and already attainable with a memory of five.

One detail remains open. The \texttt{lmvm} solver, which carries the same seed, is also flat at history five but at about $156$ iterations (\cref{tab:meshdep:lmvm-hist5}), and needs a history of $100$ to come down to $17$ (\cref{tab:meshdep:lmvm-hist100}), where seeded \texttt{bqnls} manages six with a history of five. We suspect the scalar seed rescaling, which TAO disables for \texttt{lmvm} when $J_0$ is a matrix (\cref{sec:lbfgs:tao}), but we have not confirmed this.

\section{Source-Level Comparison of the L-BFGS Implementations}
\label{sec:lbfgs-comparison}
% =============================================================================

\Cref{sec:mesh-dependence} established by experiment that installing the mass-matrix seed changes how TAO/LMVM behaves. An iteration count, though, tells us only what came out, and not what the algorithm computed to get there. This section sets the limited-memory BFGS of PETSc TAO beside the two formulations of \citet[Sect.~1.5.4]{shfp2017}, the Euclidean and the Hilbert-space, and beside the Moola reference implementation, at the level of the actual recursion, asking where an inner product enters each of them and whether the four are really doing the same thing.

There are two reasons to look this closely. The first is that the failure of \cref{sec:meshdep:tao-revisited} looked for a long time like a defect inside TAO, and an accusation of that kind should not be left resting on iteration counts. The second is that if a metric is to be handed to an optimiser at all, we need to know where in the algorithm an inner product is used, and that is a question we will want to put to a different solver in \cref{sec:precond}. The source was read against PETSc \texttt{v3.24.0} and Moola's reference L-BFGS implementation;\footnote{Specifically \texttt{src/ksp/ksp/utils/lmvm/} in PETSc and \texttt{moola/algorithms/bfgs.py} in Moola.} the structural conclusions are version-independent, though the specific line references are not.

\subsection{The common skeleton}
\label{sec:lbfgs:skeleton}

All four algorithms compute the same object, the action of an approximate inverse Hessian on the gradient, written $H_k g$. None of them ever builds $H_k$ as a matrix, because only its action on a vector is ever needed \citep[Sect.~1.5.4.2]{shfp2017}. They all compute that action the same way, by a two-loop recursion that sweeps backwards through the stored history and then forwards again.

Between the two sweeps sits a single operation, the initial inverse Hessian $H_0$, which we call the \emph{seed}. Every other operation in the recursion pairs a gradient with a step, and a pairing of that kind needs no inner product to make sense. Written so that the seed stands on its own, the shared recursion is

\begin{lstlisting}
function direction(g):           # returns -H_k g
    q = g
    for j = newest ... oldest:   # first sweep
        a[j] = rho[j] * <s[j], q>     # pairing: step . q
        q    = q - a[j] * y[j]
    z = H0(q)                    # SEED: the one metric step
    for j = oldest ... newest:   # second sweep
        b    = rho[j] * <y[j], z>     # pairing: grad-diff . z
        z    = z + (a[j] - b) * s[j]
    return -z
\end{lstlisting}

The four implementations differ only in how they fill three slots, namely the pairing, the curvature scalar $\rho$, and the seed.

\subsection{The two Schwedes formulations}
\label{sec:lbfgs:schwedes}

\Citet{shfp2017} give the recursion twice. In the Euclidean form \citep[(1.187), (1.209)]{shfp2017} the control is a coefficient vector, every pairing is the ordinary dot product, and the seed is a multiple of the identity, $H_0 = \gamma_k I$ with $\gamma_k = (s_{k-1}^{\mathsf T} y_{k-1})/(y_{k-1}^{\mathsf T} y_{k-1})$. Note that the denominator pairs two derivative objects with nothing between them.

In the Hilbert-space form \citep[(1.205), (1.210)]{shfp2017} the control lives in $\Mspace$ while the derivative lives in the dual space $\Mspace^{*}$. These are different spaces, so a pairing can no longer be a dot product. It is instead a duality pairing, a derivative evaluated on a step, written $y_k(s_k)$. The seed has more work to do as well, since it must carry an object of $\Mspace^{*}$ back into $\Mspace$, and once an inner product has been chosen the operator that does this is the Riesz map.

The two forms differ in the seed and nowhere else, and \citet[(1.208) and following]{shfp2017} says so outright. Taking $\Mspace = \R^{n}$ with the dot product makes the Riesz map the identity, and the Hilbert form collapses onto the Euclidean one. For $\Lp{\Om}$ or $H^{1}(\Om)$ controls it does not.

The hand-written reference of \cref{sec:meshdep:hilbert-lbfgs} writes the Hilbert algorithm in its \emph{primal-representer} form. It pre-applies $\Rmap_H$ to the derivative, so that $g_k := \Rmap_H(\mathrm{d}\Jt(m_k))$ is primal, and takes all pairings in $\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}{H}$. This is identical to the dual, pairing form above, since pre-applying $\Rmap_H$ and using $\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}{H}$ is the same as keeping the derivative dual and using $\Rmap_H$ only as the seed; the two are the two sides of the same coin of the Riesz representation theorem (\cref{sec:prelim:riesz})\footnote{Two of the printed equations appear to contain slips. In (1.205) the final rank-one term is printed without its denominator $y_k(s_k)$, which the primal and dual types require and which Moola's code carries; in (1.210) the last denominator is printed with $s_{k-2}$ where consistency with (1.209) calls for $y_{k-1}(s_{k-1})$.}.



\subsection{The TAO/LMVM recursion}
\label{sec:lbfgs:tao}

TAO's limited-memory driver is an ordinary quasi-Newton loop. It computes the gradient, builds a direction from the stored history, checks that the direction points downhill, runs a line search, and repeats. The history stores the step $s = X - X_{\text{prev}}$ and the gradient difference $y = F - F_{\text{prev}}$, keeping a pair only when $s^{\mathsf T} y > 0$.

Two features of the implementation obscure the recursion without changing what it computes. The BFGS direction is produced by the DFP kernel run in dual mode, which exploits a duality between those two updates, so the roles of $s$ and $y$ look reordered against a textbook two-loop even though the operator is the same. The sweeps are also batched, so that a loop of dot products becomes a single matrix-vector call.

What matters for us is what those dot products pair together. Each of them pairs the dual $y$, or the gradient, with the primal $s$, or the running vector. That is a duality pairing, and in coefficients a duality pairing is just the ordinary dot product. The bare dot is therefore the correct operation, and it carries no metric of its own.

The seed is applied as a linear solve against a matrix TAO calls $J_0$. If no $J_0$ is supplied the seed is the scalar $\gamma_k I$. If $J_0 = M_h$, which is what \texttt{Control(m, riesz\_map="L2")} installs, the solve computes $M_h^{-1} q$, so the seed is exactly the discrete $L^{2}$ Riesz map. TAO also switches off its scalar rescaling whenever $J_0$ is a full matrix rather than a diagonal one, so the Riesz seed is applied unscaled.

\subsection{The Moola recursion}
\label{sec:lbfgs:moola}

Moola keeps primal and dual vectors as separate types, so its code makes the distinction that the mathematics requires. Applying a dual object to a primal one is the duality pairing, and converting a dual object to a primal one is the Riesz map. Its recursion is the recursive form of \citet[(1.210)]{shfp2017}, with the curvature scalar $\rho = 1/y(s)$ written out explicitly, which independently confirms the denominator discussed above.

Two of its defaults are worth noting, because they are the opposite of TAO's. Moola's seed is the Riesz map already, rather than a scaled identity, and it scales that seed using the dual norm rather than the Euclidean one. It also tests convergence in the dual norm by default.

\subsection{The four implementations in pseudo-code}
\label{sec:lbfgs:instantiations}

Here are the algorithms for the four implementations, written as the skeleton of \cref{sec:lbfgs:skeleton} with each one's choices filled in.

\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
# --- Schwedes Euclidean (Eq 1.187 / 1.209) ---
function direction_schwedes_Rn(g):
    q = g                                  # g, s, y all in R^n
    for j: a[j] = (s[j].T @ q) / (y[j].T @ s[j])   # Euclidean dot
           q   -= a[j] * y[j]
    gamma_k = (s[k-1].T @ y[k-1]) / (y[k-1].T @ y[k-1])
    z = gamma_k * q                        # SEED: gamma_k * I
    for j: b   = (y[j].T @ z) / (y[j].T @ s[j])
           z  += (a[j] - b) * s[j]
    return -z
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
# --- Schwedes Hilbert (Eq 1.205 / 1.210) ---
function direction_schwedes_H(g):
    q = g                                  # g, y in M^*; s in M
    for j: a[j] = q(s[j]) / y[j](s[j])     # duality pairing
           q   -= a[j] * y[j]
    gamma_k = y[k-1](s[k-1]) / y[k-1](R_H(y[k-1]))   # dual norm
    z = gamma_k * R_H(q)                   # SEED: scaled Riesz
    for j: b   = y[j](z) / y[j](s[j])
           z  += (a[j] - b) * s[j]
    return -z
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
# --- Moola (recursive form of Eq 1.210) ---
function direction_moola(g):
    q = g                                  # dual
    for j: a[j] = s[j].apply(q) / y[j].apply(s[j])
           q.axpy(-a[j], y[j])
    theta = y[k-1].apply(s[k-1]) / y[k-1].apply(y[k-1].primal())
    z = theta * q.primal()                 # SEED: theta * Riesz
    for j: b   = y[j].apply(z) / y[j].apply(s[j])
           z.axpy(a[j] - b, s[j])
    return -z
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
# --- TAO LMVM (with Control(m, riesz_map="L2")) ---
function direction_tao_lmvm(g):
    q = g                                  # PETSc Vec of coefficients
    for j: a[j] = VecDot(s[j], q) * rho[j] # ell^2 = duality pairing
           VecAXPY(q, -a[j], y[j])
    # no scaling term: TAO disables it when J_0 is a matrix
    z = M_h^{-1} q                         # SEED: KSPSolve(J_0=M_h)
    for j: b   = VecDot(y[j], z) * rho[j]
           VecAXPY(z, a[j] - b, s[j])
    return -z
\end{lstlisting}

Reading down the column of seed lines, only those lines differ; the sweeps are structurally identical. The Schwedes-Euclidean and Schwedes-Hilbert variants differ only in whether the seed is $\gamma_k I$ or $\gamma_k \Rmap_H$, consistent with \citet[(1.208)]{shfp2017} reducing one to the other when $\Rmap$ is the identity. Moola and TAO-with-$L^{2}$ differ only in the scalar $\theta$ against an unscaled seed. Moola scales the Riesz seed by a dual-norm-correct $\theta$, while TAO uses the bare Riesz seed because its scalar rescaling is auto-disabled when $J_0$ is a matrix (\cref{sec:lbfgs:tao}).

\subsection{Correctness of the TAO/LMVM recursion}
\label{sec:lbfgs:verdict}

The recursion is structurally correct. Nowhere does it pair two dual objects together with the Riesz map missing between them, which is the error one would look for. Every pairing puts a dual object with a primal one, and the metric enters at the seed and nowhere else. With $J_0 = M_h$ the seed is the $L^{2}$ Riesz map, and TAO applies the same update as \citet[(1.210)]{shfp2017}, as Moola and as the reference of \cref{sec:meshdep:hilbert-lbfgs}, up to the seed scaling noted below. The experiments of \cref{sec:meshdep:tao-revisited} support this. There, \texttt{lmvm} carries the mass-matrix seed in every run and is mesh-independent at either history, flat at about $156$ iterations by default and at $17$ once the history is enlarged (\cref{tab:meshdep:lmvm-hist5,tab:meshdep:lmvm-hist100}). The limited-memory truncation therefore governs the cost, and the seed governs the mesh-independence, neither of which points to an inner-product bug. The related \texttt{bqnls} solver reinforces the role of the seed itself, since pyadjoint installs none for it, and it stays mesh-dependent until the seed $J_0 = M_h$ is added by hand (\cref{sec:meshdep:bqnls-isolation}), which directly shows the point of this section, that the metric enters at $H_0$.

Even so, the correctness depends on assumptions the recursion makes rather than checks. The key one is that the gradient reaches TAO as a dual object and $s$ as a primal one. The argument that \texttt{VecDot} is the right pairing fails if pyadjoint pre-applies the Riesz map and hands TAO a primal gradient, as the reference of \cref{sec:meshdep:hilbert-lbfgs} deliberately does, for then the same dots become primal-with-primal and \emph{are} missing an inverse. This is a fact about the pyadjoint interface rather than the TAO source, and is the one premise that could invert the verdict, so it is worth confirming directly. The remaining premises are milder. The Riesz solve must be exact. Here, $M_h^{-1}$ is applied by a \texttt{KSPSolve} on $J_0$, which a direct LU makes exact, whereas an iterative solve with a weak preconditioner applies a different operator and the metric is no longer clean (the same caution as for the Riesz solve in \cref{sec:meshdep:hilbert-lbfgs}). The space must be right, since $J_0 = M_h$ gives the $L^{2}$ Riesz map only, while an $H^{1}$ control needs $J_0 = M_h + K_h$, a Helmholtz solve. And the variant must be right, since the analysis is for \texttt{MATLMVMBFGS} in the \texttt{recursive} multiplication mode, whereas the \texttt{compact\_dense} and \texttt{dense} variants form Gram-type products $Y^{\mathsf T}Y$ that reintroduce cofunction-cofunction dots and would need separate checking.

Even when the algorithms are matched, residual differences remain, though these affect the iteration count rather than correctness. Moola scales its Riesz seed by a dual-norm-correct \texttt{theta} while TAO uses $M_h^{-1}$ unscaled; Moola tests convergence in the dual norm by default while TAO uses $\ell^{2}$ unless \texttt{TaoSetGradientNorm} is set, which changes \emph{when} the solver stops rather than its iterates; and, although both use a Mor\'e--Thuente strong-Wolfe line search, so this is not itself a source of difference, their default history lengths differ, five against ten. The practical failure was therefore not a correctness bug. The original inability to reproduce the Moola row (\cref{sec:meshdep:tao-revisited}) was the limited-memory history being too short for the discrete-Hessian spectrum on graded meshes, not an internal $\ell^{2}$ inner product.

Two structural points follow from this reading of the source, and both matter for \cref{sec:precond}. The first is that it is \texttt{setLMVMH0} rather than \texttt{setGradientNorm} that decides the outcome, since the seed determines the search direction and with it the mesh-dependence, whereas the gradient norm only weights the convergence test. The second is that the recursion is matrix-free apart from the seed, never assembling $H_k$, so the one genuine linear solve it performs is $M_h^{-1}$ at the seed itself. A limited-memory method therefore has no inner Krylov solve to precondition, which is why the metric has to be supplied at $H_{0}$, whereas the Newton method of \cref{sec:precond} does have one, and there the metric enters as a preconditioner instead.

\section{Two-Level Riesz-Map Preconditioning}
\label{sec:precond}
% =============================================================================

The experiments of \cref{sec:mesh-dependence} reproduced the finding of \citet{shfp2017} that a quasi-Newton optimiser becomes mesh-independent once its inner product is the Riesz map of the control space. In a limited-memory method that inner product enters as the initial-Hessian seed. All of this, though, concerns the outer optimiser alone.

This section takes up what happens one level down. A Newton method computes each search direction by solving a linear system, and that solve is itself iterative. We show that the same mesh-dependence reappears there, in the inner Krylov solve, and that the same idea removes it, namely preconditioning that solve with the Riesz map.

That an inner product can act as a preconditioner is not itself new. The idea is already present in classical operator preconditioning \citep{kirby2010, mardal2011}, and it was raised in discussion among the PETSc/TAO developers.\footnote{PETSc/TAO developers, personal communication, 2025 \citep{petsctao_discord2025}; in particular the observations that, for linear solvers, ``changing the inner product'' is exactly preconditioning, and that an inexact inner solve needs a mesh-independent preconditioner together with a preconditioned norm.} What has not been done before, to our knowledge, is to expose and remove the effect experimentally in an adjoint optimisation pipeline built on pyadjoint.

The conclusion is that the metric has to be supplied twice, as the seed on the outer iteration and as the preconditioner on the inner solve, and that mesh-independence needs both. We first expose the inner-level growth and remove it with an $L^{2}$ Riesz-map preconditioner, and we have prepared a production version of it for pyadjoint. We then show that this preconditioner must be built from the Riesz map matching the regularisation, and check that the growth left by a mismatched map is genuine conditioning rather than a side-effect of how the runs were stopped.

\subsection{A mesh-dependence hidden inside TAO/NLS}
\label{sec:precond:hidden}
The \texttt{nls} row of \cref{tab:meshdep:tao-zoo} shows two outer iterations on every mesh in the sweep and so appears mesh-independent, but actually this hides a mesh-dependence one level deeper. At each outer Newton step, \texttt{nls} solves $H p_{k} = -g_{k}$ for the search direction, where $H$ is the discrete reduced Hessian and $g_{k}$ the assembled dual gradient. That system is not solved directly but by an inner Krylov iteration, the conjugate gradient method. Written in the underlying $\ell^{2}$ coefficient inner product, $H$ is badly conditioned on graded meshes for the same reason the outer L-BFGS Hessian is (\cref{sec:meshdep:panel-a}): the cell-size scaling carried in the diagonal of the mass matrix. The inner CG iteration count per outer Newton step therefore grows with the grading, even though the outer Newton count does not. This is the problem identified by \citet{shfp2017}, repeated one level down.

To measure the hidden growth, we ran \texttt{nls} on the Poisson optimal control problem of \cref{sec:meshdep:panel-a}, on four of the six deterministic graded meshes of \cref{tab:meshdep:mesh_summary}. The inner KSP was left unpreconditioned (\texttt{-tao\_nls\_ksp\_pc\_type none}), and a counting monitor on it summed the CG iterations across both outer Newton steps. The totals are given in \cref{tab:meshdep:inner-cg-baseline}.

\subsection{Preconditioning of Krylov methods}
\label{sec:precond:definition}

The growth in \cref{tab:meshdep:inner-cg-baseline} is a conditioning problem, and the usual way to deal with poor conditioning in a Krylov solver is preconditioning. We define it here before choosing one. An iterative linear solver such as conjugate gradients does not invert the system matrix $A$ in $A x = b$; it takes a sequence of cheap steps that progressively drive the residual to zero. The number of steps it needs is governed by the \emph{conditioning} of $A$, that is, loosely by how widely its eigenvalues are spread. When the eigenvalues are tightly clustered the solver converges in a handful of steps; when they span many orders of magnitude it takes many. Preconditioning replaces the system by an equivalent one, $P^{-1} A x = P^{-1} b$, with the same solution but a better-conditioned operator, so the solver converges far faster. The preconditioner $P$ must be cheap to apply, and should approximate $A$ well enough that $P^{-1} A$ has clustered eigenvalues. Taking $P = A$ would converge in a single step but is as costly as the original problem. The trick is to capture the source of the poor conditioning while staying cheap. In our case the source of the poor conditioning is known exactly, which is what makes it possible to choose $P$ by argument rather than by experiment.

\subsection{Choosing the preconditioner}
\label{sec:precond:spectral}

We now have a problem and a general way to attack it, but not yet a choice of preconditioner. That choice does not have to be made by trial and error. The spectrum of the operator which the inner CG solves says both where the growth of \cref{tab:meshdep:inner-cg-baseline} comes from and what will remove it. The argument is the one \cref{sec:meshdep:bqnls-why} applied to the outer quasi-Newton iteration, now one level down. In the $L^{2}$ inner product the reduced Hessian is $\mathcal{H} = S^{*}S + \alpha I$, where $S$ is the control-to-state solution map. Because $S$ solves a Poisson problem it is smoothing, so $S^{*}S$ is compact and its eigenvalues decay to zero. The operator $\mathcal{H}$ is therefore the identity scaled by $\alpha$ plus a compact perturbation, and its eigenvalues cluster at $\alpha$ with only finitely many outliers. Its conditioning is bounded independently of the mesh, and that is exactly the situation in which conjugate gradients converges in a fixed number of steps.

What the inner CG sees without a preconditioner, though, is not $\mathcal{H}$ but its matrix in the $\ell^{2}$ coefficient inner product, which carries an extra factor of the mass matrix $M_h$. On a graded mesh the diagonal of $M_h$ ranges over the cell areas, so $M_h$ is badly conditioned, and this scaling spreads the spectrum out and makes the CG count grow. Preconditioning by $M_h^{-1}$, the discrete $L^{2}$ Riesz map, removes exactly this factor and returns the CG to the mesh-independent spectrum of $\mathcal{H}$ itself. The prediction is therefore a sharp one: under that preconditioner the inner-CG count should stop growing with the mesh altogether. This is the finite-element example of a general principle, namely that the natural preconditioner for a discretised operator is the discrete Riesz map of the space on which it acts \citep{kirby2010, mardal2011}.

The same picture predicts what should happen if the wrong map is chosen. Preconditioning the $L^{2}$-natural operator by the $H^{1}$ Riesz map $(M_h + K_h)^{-1}$ does not cancel the mass matrix. It over-smooths instead, replacing the clustered spectrum of $\mathcal{H}$ by one whose smallest eigenvalues shrink as the mesh resolves higher frequencies, so the count grows again. Mesh-independence returns only when the regularisation is itself an $H^{1}$ norm, because the $H^{1}$ Riesz map is then the matching one. \Cref{sec:meshdep:matching} puts both halves of this prediction to the test.

\subsection{Preconditioning the inner CG of TAO/NLS}
\label{sec:meshdep:inner-cg}
We now put that choice into practice. Preconditioning the inner CG with the discrete Riesz map $\Rmap_{L^{2}} = M_h^{-1}$ cancels the cell-size scaling that ruined the conditioning, exactly as it does for the outer optimiser in \cref{sec:meshdep:panel-a}. We first wrote this the textbook Firedrake way, as an \texttt{AuxiliaryOperatorPC} subclass returning the mass form, which should be selectable through a single options-database entry:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
'tao_nls_ksp_pc_type': 'python',
'tao_nls_ksp_pc_python_type':
    'meshdep.preconditioners.RieszMapL2',
\end{lstlisting}
This silently failed, however. The \texttt{AuxiliaryOperatorPC} needs the operator matrix to carry a Firedrake function space so that it can build the mass form on the correct space. Pyadjoint's reduced-Hessian matrix is a Python \texttt{Mat} without one, so the preconditioner was left at type \texttt{none} with no warning. The workaround was to write a plain PETSc Python preconditioner instead, \texttt{RieszMapPCContext}, which takes the function space at construction, assembles $M_h$ from it and factorises it by LU. It is wired directly onto the inner KSP after the \texttt{TAOSolver} is built:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
solver = TAOSolver(MinimizationProblem(Jhat), parameters=...)
pc = solver.tao.getKSP().getPC()
pc.setType('python')
pc.setPythonContext(RieszMapPCContext(V, riesz_map="L2"))
solver.solve()
\end{lstlisting}
The diagnostic \texttt{tao\_nls\_ksp\_view} then reported \texttt{PC type: python} with the Riesz context attached, confirming that the preconditioner was installed.

The baseline and Riesz-$L^{2}$ runs on the same four meshes are compared in \cref{tab:meshdep:inner-cg} and plotted in \cref{fig:precond-inner-cg}. Outer Newton stayed at two iterations in every row, since the Poisson problem is quadratic, so the entire row-to-row variation lies in the inner CG. The baseline totals grew $61 \to 179 \to 573 \to 1052$ across $r \in \{4, 16, 64, 128\}$, matching the shape of the SciPy $\ell^{2}$ row of \cref{tab:meshdep:panel-a}. The Riesz-$L^{2}$ totals were flat at $12$ CG iterations on every mesh in the sweep, a reduction of roughly $88\times$ at $r = 128$.

\subsection{A production-ready preconditioner for pyadjoint}
\label{sec:precond:pyadjoint}

The \texttt{RieszMapPCContext} used above takes the control function space as an explicit argument. That is fine for a single experiment, but awkward as a reusable component. The main software contribution of this work is a general version, the class \texttt{RieszMapPC}. It is a PETSc Python preconditioner attached to the reduced-Hessian Krylov solve inside TAO's Newton method. Each time that solve calls its preconditioner it hands over a dual object, a residual in $V^{*}$, and \texttt{RieszMapPC} hands back the primal representative in $V$. That is the Riesz map $V^{*}\to V$. For the $L^{2}$ metric it means solving with $M_h$, and for the $H^{1}$ metric with $M_h + K_h$. That is exactly the operation which produced the flat count of \cref{tab:meshdep:inner-cg}, now performed by the library rather than by hand. What makes it reusable is where it finds that map: it reads the Riesz map directly from each \texttt{Control} and so needs no such argument. Because it applies whichever Riesz map a control carries, the one preconditioner serves both the $L^{2}$ and the $H^{1}$ problem without modification. It is written for pyadjoint's TAO solver interface, through which Firedrake drives optimisation with PETSc TAO. It also includes a fallback, so that it can still be imported where \texttt{petsctools} is absent and fails only if it is actually used. It is selected through the two options \texttt{tao\_nls\_pc\_type = python} and \texttt{tao\_nls\_pc\_python\_type = pyadjoint.\allowbreak optimization.\allowbreak RieszMapPC}.

The preconditioner is covered by a test that installs it on TAO's inner Krylov solve. The test checks three things. First, that the preconditioner is actually attached, with the diagnostic reporting \texttt{PC type: python} and the Riesz context present. Second, that it drives the inner-CG count below the unpreconditioned baseline. Third, that it reproduces the flat count of the hand-wired \texttt{RieszMapPCContext} exactly, on the same problem. On the Poisson problem of this section it matches \cref{tab:meshdep:inner-cg} to the iteration. The preconditioner has been merged into pyadjoint, and the test into Firedrake. The two-level fix is therefore available to any pyadjoint user through the metric already attached to their \texttt{Control}, rather than through the manual plumbing of \cref{sec:meshdep:inner-cg}.

\subsection{Matching the Riesz map to the regularisation}
\label{sec:meshdep:matching}

The inner-CG preconditioning of \cref{sec:meshdep:inner-cg} used the $L^{2}$ Riesz map, which is the correct choice for the $L^{2}$-regularised problem. To see that the choice must \emph{match} the regularisation, we ran the same TAO/NLS solve on the interior random-refined meshes of \cref{sec:meshdep:meshes}, preconditioning the inner CG in turn with the $L^{2}$ map $M_h^{-1}$ and the $H^{1}$ map $(M_h + K_h)^{-1}$. The change of mesh family is deliberate. Those meshes place their refinement at random rather than by a fixed pattern, so a result that survives on them cannot come from the particular way the graded meshes are built. They also reach the same realised ratios, which keeps the two experiments comparable. The $L^{2}$-regularised results are given in \cref{tab:meshdep:matching}, and \cref{fig:precond-matching} plots them beside the mirror experiment described below.

Two checks confirm that this growth is genuine conditioning rather than a side-effect of stopping the runs early. First, the $L^{2}$- and $H^{1}$-preconditioned solves reach the \emph{same} minimiser. At the third refinement level the two objective values agree to fifteen digits ($|J^{*}_{L^{2}} - J^{*}_{H^{1}}| = 3 \times 10^{-15}$) and the controls differ in relative $L^{2}$ norm by under $10^{-6}$, while the inner-CG counts differ by a factor of twenty-five ($13$ against $323$). Second, blending the two maps by preconditioning with $M_h + w K_h$ and sweeping the stiffness weight $w$ shows that the mesh-dependence is present for \emph{every} positive weight and disappears only at $w = 0$. At the finest mesh the count falls from $672$ at $w = 1$ to $73$ at $w = 10^{-4}$, but each fixed positive $w$ still grows with the mesh, and only the pure $L^{2}$ map is flat. The $H^{1}$ term is therefore genuinely the wrong norm for this problem, not just a poorly scaled one.

The reverse holds when the regularisation is changed to match. On an $H^{1}$-regularised problem (the objective of \cref{sec:meshdep:panel-b}), the roles reverse. The $H^{1}$ Riesz map keeps the inner-CG count flat, between $8$ and $10$ across the sweep, while the $L^{2}$ map now grows, from $370$ to the $10^{4}$-iteration cap. This is the inner-CG counterpart of the outer-optimiser result of \cref{sec:meshdep:panel-b}. It makes the same point from the preconditioning side. The Riesz map that delivers mesh-independence is the one matching the norm in which the control is regularised.

\subsection{Use in a pyadjoint problem}
\label{sec:precond:usage}

The two-level preconditioning developed above needs none of the manual plumbing of \cref{sec:meshdep:inner-cg} to reuse. On the outer optimiser the metric is selected by the \texttt{riesz\_map} argument of the pyadjoint \texttt{Control}; on the inner Krylov solve the production-ready \texttt{RieszMapPC} is selected through two PETSc options. A complete inner-preconditioned Newton solve of a control problem reads:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
from firedrake.adjoint import Control, ReducedFunctional
from pyadjoint import MinimizationProblem
from pyadjoint.optimization.tao_solver import TAOSolver

c = Control(m, riesz_map="L2")          # metric on the outer optimiser
Jhat = ReducedFunctional(J, c)

solver = TAOSolver(MinimizationProblem(Jhat), {
    "tao_type": "nls",
    "tao_nls_pc_type": "python",
    "tao_nls_pc_python_type":
        "pyadjoint.optimization.RieszMapPC",
})
m_opt = solver.solve()
\end{lstlisting}
The single argument \texttt{riesz\_map="L2"} sets the outer metric, and the two \texttt{tao\_nls\_pc} options attach the same Riesz map as the inner-CG preconditioner. Changing both to \texttt{"H1"} switches the whole solve to the $H^{1}$ metric with no other change, which is how the matched and mismatched runs of \cref{sec:meshdep:matching} were produced.

The gain over the research prototype is more than brevity. In \cref{sec:meshdep:inner-cg} the preconditioner had to be given the function space explicitly, and then wired onto the inner Krylov object by hand once the solver had been built. That asks the user to know how TAO assembles its Newton step. Here the metric is declared once, on the \texttt{Control}, which is where the statement of the problem already lives. Both levels of the solve follow from it, and none of TAO's internals appear in the user's code.

None of this costs flexibility. The preconditioner reads the map from the control rather than fixing one, so the same code serves the $L^{2}$ and the $H^{1}$ problem without modification. Because it is chosen from the PETSc options database, the solver type, the inner Krylov method and the tolerances can all be varied around it, and it can be switched off without editing the program. It also announces itself under \texttt{-tao\_view}, naming the Riesz map it applies, so the choice is visible in the solver's own diagnostics rather than buried in the calling code. Given an operator it cannot precondition, it raises an error, in contrast to the \texttt{AuxiliaryOperatorPC} route of \cref{sec:meshdep:inner-cg}, which was left unset with no warning at all.

\subsection{Nonlinear conjugate gradient and the metric}
\label{sec:precond:cg}

The metric reaches a TAO solve by one of two routes. It can enter as the initial-Hessian seed of a limited-memory quasi-Newton method (\cref{sec:meshdep:tao-revisited}), or as a preconditioner on the inner Krylov solve of a Newton method (\cref{sec:meshdep:inner-cg}). The nonlinear conjugate gradient solver \texttt{cg} is the one strongly mesh-dependent row of \cref{tab:meshdep:tao-zoo}, and it offers neither. The reason is structural rather than a matter of tuning.

\texttt{cg} is not a limited-memory method, so it stores no curvature pairs and forms no approximate inverse Hessian. There is therefore no seed for the metric to enter through, which is the route \texttt{lmvm} and \texttt{bqnls} use. It also performs no Hessian solve. Asking TAO for its Krylov object returns nothing, where \texttt{nls} returns the very solve that \cref{sec:meshdep:inner-cg} preconditioned. What is left is the search direction itself, formed as $-g_{k} + \beta_{k} d_{k-1}$ from the assembled dual gradient, which is used directly as though it were a primal direction. TAO exposes no nonlinear-preconditioning interface at which the Riesz map could be inserted there.

We ran \texttt{cg} on the Poisson problem for a fixed budget of forty iterations, once with the $L^{2}$ Riesz map on the control and once with the Euclidean one, and did the same for \texttt{lmvm}. The objectives are given in \cref{tab:precond:cg-metric}. The two \texttt{cg} runs agree in every digit, while \texttt{lmvm} returns a different value, because its metric enters through the seed. The Riesz map therefore never reaches \texttt{cg}'s search directions, which is what the missing points of attachment predict.

A line search that failed to find good steps could produce the same growth. With its default Mor\'e--Thuente line search \texttt{cg} converged in $68$ iterations on a uniform mesh, and with the line search disabled, so that unit steps are taken, it did not converge at all. Unlike a Newton method \texttt{cg} has no natural unit step, so the line search is essential and is doing its job.

The mesh-dependence of \texttt{cg} is therefore neither a bug nor a preconditioner left unset. It is built into the algorithm as TAO exposes it, which works in the underlying $\ell^{2}$ inner product and offers no interface at which the function-space metric could be supplied. Making \texttt{cg} mesh-robust would need the algorithm-level treatment given to LMVM, putting the inner product into the recursion itself, rather than either of the two mechanisms used in this dissertation.

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% =============================================================================

This work has developed the mathematical and numerical infrastructure for adjoint-based optimal control of two model problems. We derived the weak formulation, the adjoint equation and the $L^2$-gradient of the reduced functional for the linear Poisson problem in \cref{sec:poisson-weak,sec:poisson-optimisation,sec:poisson-derivative}, and verified the resulting numerical pipeline through manufactured solutions and Taylor tests in \cref{sec:poisson-numerics}. We then extended the same machinery to the nonlinear, time-dependent scalar viscous Burgers problem in \cref{sec:burgers-weak,sec:burgers-derivative,sec:burgers-numerics}, showing that the structure of the adjoint analysis carries over to a problem whose adjoint must be solved backward in time.

\Cref{sec:mesh-dependence} reproduced Panel~(a) of Table~2.2 of \citet{shfp2017} on deterministic graded meshes spanning ratios from $4$ to $64$. A custom implementation of BFGS-in-Hilbert-space following \citet[Sect.~1.5.4.1]{shfp2017}, in which every dot product in the two-loop recursion is taken in the $L^{2}$ inner product, produced an iteration count that was constant at six across the entire range of mesh ratios. The SciPy L-BFGS-B row, run on the ambient $\ell^{2}$ inner product with the convergence criterion checked externally in $L^{2}$, produced an iteration count that doubled at every refinement, in agreement with the polynomial bound derived in \citet[Sect.~2.2.4]{shfp2017}. Routing the same problem through PETSc TAO/LMVM with \texttt{Control(m, riesz\_map="L2")} also reproduces the broad pattern, provided that the LMVM history size is enlarged from the PETSc default of five (which is too small to capture the spectrum of the discrete Hessian on the strongly graded meshes); the diagnostic chain that established this, including a noise-floor probe at machine precision, is documented in \cref{sec:meshdep:tao-revisited,sec:meshdep:tao-zoo}. Panel~(b) of Table~2.2, which places the $H^{1}$ inner product on the control space, was reproduced once we recognised that its objective regularises in the $H^{1}$ norm to match the metric. On the correspondingly $H^{1}$-regularised problem the Hilbert-space L-BFGS is mesh-independent at four iterations across the sweep, matching the constant four of \citet{shfp2017}, and the large counts seen earlier came from applying the $H^{1}$ metric to the $L^{2}$-regularised problem rather than from any deficiency of the $H^{1}$ Riesz map.

The same mesh-dependence reappears one level deeper, in the inner conjugate-gradient solve of TAO's Newton method, and is removed in the same way, by preconditioning that solve with the $L^{2}$ Riesz map. The total inner-CG count falls from $1052$ to a flat $12$ at the highest mesh ratio. A production version of this preconditioner, which reads its Riesz map from each \texttt{Control}, has been merged into pyadjoint as \texttt{RieszMapPC}.

Several directions remain open. The vector-valued time-dependent Burgers problem treated by \citet[Sect.~1.4.5]{shfp2017} would be a natural extension of \cref{sec:burgers-derivative}, and would supply the nonlinear, time-dependent test problem that \cref{sec:meshdep:tao-zoo} identified as missing from the survey. A second direction is to use the inner-product-aware optimisation framework on a problem with practical relevance, for example tidal-turbine array layout optimisation, as in \citet[Chap.~3]{shfp2017}. A third direction, on the implementation side, is to extend the experiment code with Newton-CG and IPOPT wrappers so that the full grid of methods considered by \citet{shfp2017} is reproducible. A fourth is to close the remaining empirical gap in \cref{sec:lbfgs-comparison}, whose source-level argument predicts mesh-independence for TAO/LMVM but is demonstrated here only on the graded meshes; confirming it on the randomly refined meshes of \citet[Sect.~2.3.2]{shfp2017}, which reach ratios of order $10^{5}$, would test it far harder.
